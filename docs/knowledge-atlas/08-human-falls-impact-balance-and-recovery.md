# 08 · Human Falls, Impact, Balance, and Recovery

This file documents how balance is controlled, how it fails, and what the body does about it: the sensory systems of equilibrium, the mechanics of slips and trips, the phases of a fall, protective responses, landing and force attenuation, and the recovery movements that end the event. It depends on the vestibular and proprioceptive structures of [04-human-anatomy-and-physiology.md](04-human-anatomy-and-physiology.md), the COM-BOS mechanics of [05-human-biomechanics-and-locomotion.md](05-human-biomechanics-and-locomotion.md), and the action-ontology format of [06-human-actions-gestures-and-motion.md](06-human-actions-gestures-and-motion.md).

## 1. Scope and Framing: Falls as Movement Events

A fall is, mechanically, an uncontrolled transfer of the body's centre of mass beyond its base of support followed by an impact the body must manage. This file treats falls as movement events with phases, responses, and outcomes — descriptive structure-and-mechanism knowledge throughout. Consequences are described only at population level with age-related factors recorded as variation, not as diagnosis; nothing here is clinical guidance, risk assessment for individuals, or instruction (safety boundary of 04 §1). Trained fall techniques exist in martial arts and safety training; the atlas describes their mechanics without recommending practice.

## 2. Balance Control: Sensory Inputs and Postural Strategies

Quiet standing is active control, not rest. The controller tracks the **centre of mass (COM)** against the **centre of pressure (COP)** — the point where the net ground reaction acts — and steers the COP to keep the COM inside the **limits of stability**, the region of COM positions from which balance can still be recovered without stepping or grasping. Three sensory streams feed this loop. The **vestibular system** (04 §8) measures head angular acceleration in the semicircular canals and linear acceleration plus gravity-relative tilt in the otolith organs, giving the fastest absolute reference. **Vision** supplies optical flow, horizon reference, and obstacle information. **Proprioception and cutaneous input** report body configuration and the state of the contact surface. The system **reweights** these inputs by context: on a compliant or moving surface, proprioceptive information degrades and vestibular and visual weight rises; in darkness or on unreliable visual fields (moving escalators, virtual displays), vision is downweighted. Reweighting explains why environmental conditions change balance performance without any change in the person.

Automatic postural responses scale to the perturbation. The **ankle strategy** — a distal-to-proximal sway counter via calf and hip activation — handles slow, small perturbations on firm surfaces. The **hip strategy** — rapid trunk and hip flexion-extension torques — handles faster or larger perturbations and compliant surfaces where ankle torque cannot push effectively. The **stepping strategy** recruits a new base of support when the perturbation exceeds what in-place torques can absorb; it is the fastest large-recovery option and dominates real-world recoveries. Ahead of any voluntary movement, **anticipatory postural adjustments** pre-activate posture muscles so that a lifted arm or approaching step does not first destabilise the body (04 §6). Failure is usually a matter of margin and time: how far the COM has left the limits of stability, and how much recovery time the perturbation leaves.

## 3. Perturbation Mechanics: Slips, Trips, and Other Losses

Balance-loss events differ mechanically by how the perturbation enters the body. A **slip** is a friction failure: the support foot slides (typically forward) beneath the body, so the base of support runs out from under a COM that continues forward — the body enters **backward balance loss** (COM behind the feet), the harder direction for many people to recover from because the large hip extensors work across a falling-back configuration. Recovery demands rapid rearward stepping or squatted arrest. A **trip** is an obstruction failure: the swing foot strikes an obstacle, the blocked foot becomes a pivot, and forward momentum rotates the body about it — **forward balance loss** with the COM accelerating ahead of the base. Recovery demands large, fast forward steps or grasping (entry A08-02). Pushes, uneven ground, and unstable objects (a rolling stone, a collapsing box) produce mixed directions; the direction of COM travel relative to the base is the invariant that determines which recovery response fits. These mechanics connect directly to 05 §2 (friction, COM, BOS) and to environmental design, which the atlas records only as mechanism.

## 4. Fall Phases and Protective Responses

Falls unfold in four phases. **Initiation**: the perturbation or voluntary loss begins — a slip's shear failure, a trip's contact, a push, a faint, or a deliberate descent. **Descent**: the COM accelerates under gravity while the body has one response window; voluntary reaction alone commonly takes ~150-250 ms, a full balance-recovery movement several hundred ms more, and a body falling from standing height reaches the ground in roughly 0.6-0.75 s (all approximate) — so the window is the binding constraint, and responses compete with it. **Impact**: the first body segment contacts ground or object, transferring kinetic energy as an impulse; which segment strikes first, and at what velocity, depends on the descent trajectory and any arrest already achieved. **Arrest**: motion is dissipated — by protective responses, by energy-spreading techniques, or by the surface itself — and the event ends in recovery, in rest on the ground, or in continued sliding.

**Protective responses** are the body's standard repertoire during descent and at impact. **Protective stepping** — one to many rapid steps, forward, backward, or sideways — rebuilds the base of support under the COM and is the most common effective recovery. **Protective arm extension** reaches the hands toward the approaching surface or toward available supports; reachable grasp points (rails, furniture) are the fastest effective arrest when present, and where the hands strike the ground they reduce head and trunk impact velocity at the cost of loading the outstretched arm — a population-level trade recorded descriptively here. **Grasping** responses extend this to clutching fixed objects. **Lowering responses** — squatting, controlled sitting down — reduce the fall height and therefore the impact energy. All of these are phase-structured, time-constrained actions; entry A08-01 and A08-02 record two of them in schema form.

## 5. Landing Mechanics and Force Attenuation

Impact severity is an impulse problem: the body arrives with a fixed momentum, and the **peak force scales inversely with the stopping time and distance** over which the momentum is cancelled (16-physics; OpenStax *College Physics*). A **stiff landing** — straight legs, short arrest distance — cancels the same momentum in a short time and produces high peak forces, several times body weight even from modest heights (approximate; scales with height and surface). A **soft landing** — sequential flexion at ankle, knee, and hip under eccentric control — extends the deceleration over distance and time, cutting peak forces substantially and distributing the load across leg muscle groups; landing technique is largely a matter of how much compliance the joints are allowed to give (08 §4 entries A08-04; 07 §9 for performance contexts).

**Rolling** extends the same logic spatially: by distributing contact across successive body segments (feet, then seat, then rounded back for a backward roll; sequential hand-forearm-shoulder contact for a shoulder roll), the total impulse is spread over a longer path and time, lowering every local peak. The **parachute landing fall**, developed for parachute descents, is the systematic version of this idea — contact flows through feet, calf, thigh, hip, and lateral trunk in sequence with the limbs flexed, spreading the landing impulse along the body's side. Its mechanics — contact sequencing, limb flexion, momentum redirection — are documented here at form level; training it belongs to qualified instruction.

## 6. Recovery Movements: Arrest, Getting Up, and Regaining Balance

After arrest, the event ends one of two ways. **Regaining balance** means re-centring: a series of small steps or sways brings the COM back inside a stable base, posture resets, and activity resumes; this is the normal endpoint of the vast majority of stumbles (population-level framing). **Getting up from the ground** is a composite transition using the action ontology of 06 §2: the standard sequence families are roll to side → push through hands and knees → half-kneel → stand, or via a side-sit route; furniture, rails, or a stable chair are commonly used as supports, and support use increases with age as a population trend with wide individual spread. The mechanically hardest steps are the push-to-sitting (trunk lift against gravity through the arms) and the final half-kneel-to-stand (single-leg strength on a narrow base). **Martial-arts-style backward roll arrest** (entry A08-03) inverts the getting-up sequence into a fall-ending technique: the body rounds, contacts the ground sequentially, and redirects momentum back to the feet. These recovery movements are documented descriptively; individual capability varies widely and nothing here is an exercise prescription.

## 7. Fall Consequences and Biomechanical Failure Modes (Population-Level)

At impact, standing-height falls can generate peak forces of several kilonewtons — several times body weight — depending on surface stiffness, response, and contact segment (approximate; OpenStax *College Physics* impulse mechanics). Population-level injury statistics consistently show that commonly impacted regions include the hip and pelvis, the distal forearm and wrist (protective arm extension), the shoulder, and the head; which regions dominate varies with descent direction, height, and response type. Age-related factors are recorded here strictly as variation: population averages show slower reaction times, reduced muscle strength and power, altered sensory weighting, and changed bone and tissue tolerance with advancing age — averages with large spread, and many older individuals outperform the average for their age group. None of this supports individual inference; it is mechanism and statistics, not diagnosis.

**Biomechanical failure modes** explain when protective responses do not succeed: **insufficient time**, when reaction plus movement time exceeds the response window the perturbation leaves; **wrong strategy selection**, as when an ankle strategy attempts to arrest a perturbation too large for in-place torque; **strength and range limits**, when the required eccentric braking or stepping power exceeds available muscle output; **base and surface failures**, when friction, obstacles, or clutter make any step strategy unsafe; and **attention limits**, when dual-task load delays response initiation. Each failure mode is a mechanical statement about margin, and each maps to a phase of §4 where it bites.

### A08-01 · Forward slip recovery step

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Name | Forward slip recovery step |
| Class | fall-response |
| Definition | Recovery from a forward foot slip in which the support foot slides ahead, the COM drops behind the base (backward balance loss), and rapid rearward stepping rebuilds a base of support under the COM. |
| Body segments | Both lower limbs, pelvis, trunk; arms react freely to aid balance. |
| Joint actions | Rapid hip and knee flexion of the slipping limb to shorten and reposition it; rearward step placement; trunk adjustment over the new base (approximate). |
| Phase structure | Slip onset (shear failure at foot-surface interface) → COM lags behind base → rapid limb recovery and rearward stepping → re-established base → re-centring. |
| Temporal profile | Unfolds within roughly 0.5-1 s from slip onset (approximate); the response window is the binding constraint. |
| Kinetic profile | Large, fast hip-extensor and stepping moments; friction at the foot-surface interface determines slip onset and duration; multiple recovery steps are common. |
| Context | Wet, icy, polished, or loose surfaces; footwear and surface pairing drive likelihood. |
| Variation | Step count, direction, and depth vary; squatting arrest substitutes for stepping in some individuals; responses differ with training and age as population trends. |
| Safety note | Descriptive mechanics only; not a fall-risk assessment or personal safety instruction. |
| Sources | OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e* (chs. 10, 14); OpenStax *College Physics* (friction, impulse); NCBI Bookshelf (balance and falls entries). |

### A08-02 · Trip recovery with protective arm reach

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Name | Trip recovery with protective arm reach |
| Class | fall-response |
| Definition | Response to a trip in which the swing foot is obstructed and the body rotates forward about it (forward balance loss), answered by rapid forward stepping combined with arm extension toward surfaces or supports. |
| Body segments | Both lower limbs, pelvis, trunk, both arms and hands. |
| Joint actions | Large rapid forward step with hip/knee extension drive; shoulder flexion with elbow extension reaching hands toward ground or grasp targets; trunk extension braking the forward pitch (approximate). |
| Phase structure | Obstruction of swing foot → forward momentum about the blocked foot → forward stepping and/or reaching → arrest at supports, at ground contact with arm loading, or at full descent. |
| Temporal profile | Roughly 0.5-1 s to first contact or successful step (approximate). |
| Kinetic profile | Forward angular momentum about the foot must be braked; hands reaching a support deliver the fastest effective arrest; ground-reaching hands accept impact loading at the outstretched arm (population-level trade). |
| Context | Cluttered floors, uneven pavements, stairs, rugs and cables; obstacles of any height. |
| Variation | Elevating (high-step) versus lowering (long-step) stepping styles; single versus multiple steps; grasp success depends entirely on reachable supports; responses vary with age and training as population trends. |
| Safety note | Descriptive mechanics only; not a fall-risk assessment or personal safety instruction. |
| Sources | OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e* (chs. 10, 14); OpenStax *College Physics* (rotational dynamics, impulse); NCBI Bookshelf (balance and falls entries). |

### A08-03 · Backward roll fall arrest (martial-arts style)

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Name | Backward roll fall arrest (martial-arts style) |
| Class | fall-response |
| Definition | Trained backward fall response in which the body rounds and contacts the ground sequentially from pelvis through the curved back, head kept clear, redirecting momentum to return to the feet or come to rest. |
| Body segments | Whole body; arms slap or brace; spine rounds; head flexed. |
| Joint actions | Hip and knee flexion lowering toward the ground; chin tuck and trunk flexion rounding the back; sequential ground contact pelvis to upper back; arms extend to spread or slap contact; hips and knees drive the momentum over into a stand (approximate). |
| Phase structure | Backward loss onset → crouch and lowering → seated contact → sequential back contact with momentum redirect → recovery to feet or rest. |
| Temporal profile | Typically under ~1 s for the contact sequence (approximate). |
| Kinetic profile | Impulse-spreading: distributing contact over time and distance lowers peak forces relative to a rigid backward fall; the rounded back and tucked head keep the skull off the contact path (impulse-momentum mechanics, 16-physics). |
| Context | Martial arts breakfall training, judo and related arts, safety training contexts; mats typical. |
| Variation | Side breakfall and forward-roll variants; trained responses differ from spontaneous protective responses in timing and shape; execution quality varies with training and is not assessed here. |
| Safety note | Describes mechanics of a trained technique only; learning it belongs to qualified instruction, and the atlas gives no participation advice. |
| Sources | OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e* (chs. 10, 11); OpenStax *College Physics* (impulse and momentum); NCBI Bookshelf. |

### A08-04 · Soft landing from a drop

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Name | Soft landing from a drop |
| Class | fall-response |
| Definition | Controlled descent landing, as from a step or low platform, in which sequential ankle-knee-hip flexion extends the stopping distance and time to absorb momentum with low peak force. |
| Body segments | Both lower limbs, pelvis, trunk; arms contribute balance. |
| Joint actions | Initial contact at ball of foot then full foot; eccentric ankle, knee, and hip flexion sequenced to decelerate; trunk leaned to keep load over the base (approximate, height-dependent). |

| Temporal profile | Absorption phase ~0.2-0.5 s (approximate); total landing under a second. |

| Context | Steps, curbs, platforms, gym mats, play and sport environments. |
| Variation | Drop height, footwear, surface compliance, and technique depth change force magnitudes; stiff-landing patterns occur normally when surprise removes preparation; individual technique varies widely. |
