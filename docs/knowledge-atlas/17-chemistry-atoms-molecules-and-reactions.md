# 17. Chemistry: Atoms, Molecules and Reactions

Chemistry explains matter through its atomic and molecular structure: what things are made of, how they bond, and how they transform. It sits between physics, which supplies the underlying forces, and biology, materials and earth science, which consume its explanations. This guide states the established core of general and organic chemistry and marks its links to the movement, materials, earth and ecology domains.

## 1. Scope and role in the atlas

The guide covers atomic structure, periodic-table logic, bonding and intermolecular forces, reaction types and stoichiometry, equilibrium, organic chemistry and biomolecules, states of matter, water chemistry, and the energetics of reactions. It grounds the materials guide (18), explains geochemical mechanisms for earth science (19), and supplies the biochemical vocabulary used by biology-linked guides.

## 2. Atomic structure: protons, neutrons, electrons

An atom consists of a nucleus of protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral), surrounded by electrons (negative) occupying orbitals of defined energy. The atomic number Z counts protons and defines the element; the mass number A counts protons plus neutrons. In a neutral atom, electron count equals proton count. Electrons determine chemical behaviour: valence electrons — those in the outermost shell — form bonds, and atoms tend toward configurations in which valence shells are filled.

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different neutron counts: carbon-12 and carbon-14 both have Z = 6 but differ in mass. Chemically, isotopes of an element behave almost identically; physically, some are radioactive and decay at element-characteristic, measurable rates, which is the basis of radiometric dating used in geology (19) and of metabolic tracing in biology.

## 3. Periodic table logic

The periodic table orders elements by atomic number so that chemical behaviour repeats periodically, because valence-electron configurations repeat. Columns (groups) share valence counts and therefore similar chemistry: alkali metals (one valence electron, highly reactive), halogens (seven, strongly electron-seeking), noble gases (full shells, nearly inert). Rows (periods) fill successive shells. Two graded properties organize reactivity: atomic radius shrinks across a period as nuclear pull on the valence shell grows, and grows down a group as shells are added; electronegativity — the pull an atom exerts on shared electrons — likewise increases across a period and decreases down a group. These trends predict bond type and polarity without memorising individual cases.

## 4. Bonding and intermolecular forces

Ionic bonding: a metal transfers electrons to a nonmetal; oppositely charged ions then attract electrostatically, forming brittle, high-melting crystals that conduct only when dissolved or molten. Covalent bonding: atoms share electron pairs, forming discrete molecules (as in water) or extended networks (as in diamond). Metallic bonding: atoms release valence electrons into a shared "sea", which is why metals conduct electricity and heat and deform without shattering — the structure-property link the materials guide (18) develops.

Covalent bonds may be polar: when the two atoms differ in electronegativity, the shared electrons sit closer to one atom, giving partial charges. Molecular shape (from electron-pair repulsion) determines whether these partial charges cancel; symmetry can leave a molecule nonpolar overall. Between molecules, weaker forces govern phase behaviour and solubility: hydrogen bonding (a strong dipole interaction when hydrogen bonds to N, O or F — the reason water is liquid at room temperature and ice floats), and van der Waals forces, including temporary-dipole dispersion forces present in all matter and dominant in nonpolar substances. These intermolecular forces, not the strong covalent bonds, are what break when things melt, boil or dissolve.

## 5. Chemical reactions, stoichiometry and equilibrium

Reactions rearrange atoms; mass is conserved, so balanced equations carry the bookkeeping. Stoichiometry converts between mass and amount via the mole, Avogadro's number 6.022×10^23 particles per mole, and molar mass; a balanced equation's coefficients give mole ratios, from which limiting reactants and yields follow.

Common reaction classes: synthesis (A + B → AB), decomposition (AB → A + B), combustion (fuel + oxygen → oxides + heat), single and double displacement, acid-base neutralisation (acid + base → salt + water), and redox reactions, in which electrons transfer and oxidation states change — the electron bookkeeping behind combustion, corrosion and biological respiration.

Many reactions run both directions. At equilibrium, forward and reverse rates are equal, so concentrations stop changing — not because reaction stopped but because the two directions cancel. Le Chatelier's principle states, conceptually, that a system at equilibrium shifts to counteract an imposed change: adding reactant pushes the reaction forward; removing a product pulls it forward; changing temperature shifts it in the direction that absorbs or releases heat accordingly. This principle explains carbonate buffering in the ocean and oxygen binding in blood.

## 6. Organic chemistry basics

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. Carbon's four bonds and catenation (bonding to itself in chains and rings) generate structural variety unmatched by other elements. Hydrocarbon classes: alkanes (single bonds, saturated), alkenes (double bonds), alkynes (triple bonds), aromatics (ring systems such as benzene with delocalised electrons). Functional groups are bonded atom clusters that confer characteristic reactivity: hydroxyl (alcohols), carbonyl (aldehydes, ketones), carboxyl (acids), amino (amines), ester and others.

Polymers are long chains of repeated units; synthetic plastics and biological macromolecules are both polymers, differing in monomer sequence and precision. The four biomolecule classes are organic chemistry in biological service: carbohydrates (sugars and starches — energy storage and structure), proteins (amino-acid chains folded into shapes that catalyse and build), lipids (fats, oils and membranes — dense energy storage and barriers), and nucleic acids (DNA and RNA — information polymers). The biology domain guide develops their cellular roles; their chemistry — hydrolysis, condensation, hydrogen bonding that drives folding — is documented here.

## 7. States of matter and phase changes

Solids hold shape through fixed arrangements (crystalline) or disorder (glassy); liquids flow but hold volume through close contact; gases fill their container with negligible intermolecular contact. Phase changes are intermolecular-force events: melting overcomes enough ordering to allow flow; vaporisation separates molecules entirely; condensation and freezing release energy because molecules settle into lower-energy arrangements. Energy absorbed or released at constant temperature during a phase change is latent heat — the reason sweating cools the body and evaporating water cools the air, mechanisms the earth guide (19) re-uses for clouds and storms. Pressure shifts phase boundaries; phase diagrams map solid, liquid and gas regions and the triple point where all three coexist.

## 8. Water chemistry: solvent, pH, buffers

Water's polarity and hydrogen bonding make it an exceptional solvent: it surrounds and separates ions (hydrating them) and dissolves polar molecules, while nonpolar substances (oils) are excluded — the hydrophobic effect that also drives protein folding and membrane formation. Solid water is less dense than liquid water because hydrogen bonds lock molecules into an open lattice; ice floats, insulating water below, with major consequences for aquatic life.

pH quantifies acidity: pH = −log10 of the hydronium ion concentration; each unit is a factor of ten. Acids donate protons, bases accept them. A buffer is a weak acid with its conjugate base (or the reverse): it consumes added acid or base, holding pH nearly constant. Natural buffers regulate blood pH and ocean pH; the carbonate system links atmospheric carbon dioxide to ocean acidity, which is why the earth (19) and ecology (20) guides borrow this chemistry.

## 9. Energy in reactions: exothermic, endothermic, catalysis

Chemical energy is stored in bonds and molecular arrangement. Reactions that release energy to the surroundings are exothermic (combustion, most oxidation); reactions that absorb energy are endothermic (photosynthesis, many decompositions). Energy changes are reported as ΔH: negative for exothermic, positive for endothermic.

Energy release or absorption, however, does not determine speed. Reactant bonds must first be strained or broken; the energy barrier en route is the activation energy. Rate rises with temperature (more molecules exceed the barrier) and with concentration or pressure (more collisions). A catalyst provides an alternative reaction pathway with lower activation energy; it speeds both forward and reverse directions and changes neither the ΔH nor the final equilibrium — only how fast equilibrium is reached. Enzymes are biological catalysts: protein molecules whose active sites lower activation barriers with molecular precision, enabling metabolism at mild temperatures; their kinetics is the biological specialisation of the chemistry stated here.
