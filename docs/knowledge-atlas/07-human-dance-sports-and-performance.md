# 07 · Human Dance, Sports, and Performance

This file documents stylised movement systems — dance forms, sport movement families, martial arts families, and performance acrobatics — at biomechanics level. These traditions specialise the general primitives of [05-human-biomechanics-and-locomotion.md](05-human-biomechanics-and-locomotion.md) and the everyday actions of [06-human-actions-gestures-and-motion.md](06-human-actions-gestures-and-motion.md) into codified vocabularies.

## 1. Scope and Reading Guide: Stylised Movement Systems at Form Level

Stylised movement differs from everyday movement in that its form is itself the goal: positions, lines, timing, and stylistic signatures are trained deliberately. The atlas describes all forms here through one lens — **biomechanics** — and one level — **technique families** rather than school-specific codifications. Performance traditions vary across cultures, communities, and schools; this file describes movement mechanics, never cultural authority, ownership, or value judgements between traditions. Where a form carries cultural origins (street dance, folk forms, martial arts), those origins are acknowledged and left to their communities.

Every technique below decomposes into the same components the atlas uses everywhere: joint actions from 04 §4, torque and COM mechanics from 05 §2, and phase structure in the controlled vocabulary of 01 §3. Contact sports, lifting, and acrobatics involve real risk in practice; entries describe mechanics only, and training belongs to qualified instruction.

## 2. Ballet: Turnout, Positions, and Verticality

Ballet trains movement around external rotation and vertical alignment. **Turnout** is external hip rotation of both legs so the feet point away from each other; the functional ideal approaches 180° combined, but achievable turnout is limited by hip anatomy and varies widely between dancers — forcing range beyond one's anatomy is a documented risk area, recorded here as mechanics, not coaching. The **positions of the feet** (first through fifth, degrees of overlap with heels together through heel-to-toe contact) and **positions of the arms (port de bras)** codify shapes that keep the trunk tall and the shoulders quiet. A **plié** (entry A07-01) is the controlled knee bend with heels grounded and turnout preserved; **relevé** raises the heel onto the ball of the foot through ankle plantarflexion, loading the calf complex; **pointe work** extends this to the extreme plantarflexed foot on its platform, one of the highest ankle-foot demands in any movement tradition.

Turns illustrate ballet's mechanics: a **pirouette** gains angular momentum from a push-off (often a shallow plié-to-relevé drive against the floor), then conserves it on one supporting leg while arm positions regulate the moment of inertia; repeated **spotting** — snapping the head around to fixate a point each revolution — re-anchors the visual field and limits the vestibular disturbance of spinning (04 §8). Throughout, ballet adds verticality and line to the same COM-BOS physics documented in 05 §2.

## 3. Contemporary, Ballroom, and Street Styles

**Contemporary dance** organises movement around weight, momentum, and the floor: release technique lets segments fall and rebound rather than holding shape, floor work transitions through seated, kneeling, and inverted configurations, and suspension-and-fall phrasing plays the body's decelerations as content. Mechanically it is a study in controlled free fall and eccentric arrest — the vocabulary of 05 §6 and 08 §5 repurposed as art.

**Ballroom and partner dances** organise two bodies as one system. A **frame** — connected arms and torso tone between partners — transmits small pressure and timing changes rather than large forces, so **lead and follow** work as a low-bandwidth communication channel: the leader initiates direction changes through frame displacement and weight cues; the follower maintains tone that neither collapses nor resists, converting cues into steps while preserving own-axis balance. Travelling patterns (for example waltz-style progression) chain rotational and translational steps with rise and fall; the couple's shared COM management supersedes each individual's.

**Hip-hop and street styles** organise around **groove** — a continuous, bouncing weight shift that keeps the body in rhythmic motion between accents — and **isolation**, the ability to move one body region (head, shoulders, chest, hips) while neighbours stay quiet, which is fine segmental control of the trunk described in 04 §5. **Breaking** layers toprock (standing rhythmic steps), downrock or footwork (floor-supported patterns on hands and feet), freezes (held static shapes, often inverted), and power moves (rotational, acrobatic elements) — the last overlapping the acrobatics of §8. Popping and locking add sharp segmental accents and held contrasts to the groove. These forms are documented at mechanics level with respect for their cultural origins.

## 4. Folk, Classical, and Traditional Forms at Form Level

Across the world's folk and classical traditions, movement vocabularies share recurring mechanical families, which is what the atlas records. **Rhythmic step vocabularies** chain a small set of footwork cells (steps, taps, stamps, pivots) into repeating patterns synced to music. **Group formations** — circles, lines, processions — add relational geometry: dancers manage shared spacing while executing personal footwork. **Prop work** extends the reach-grasp chain to scarves, sticks, ribbons, and fans, multiplying tool-use coordination from 06 §3. Classical stage traditions add codified posture and gesture systems — stylised arm paths, expressive hand positions, trained facial and eye work — which the atlas describes as movement structure only, without interpreting meaning or claiming authority over any tradition. Depth of documentation here is deliberately form-level: enough mechanics to recognise the movement family, no codification claims.

## 5. Sports Movement Families: Striking, Throwing, and Athletics

**Striking actions** — punches, kicks, and implement strikes — generate impact from a proximal-to-distal sequence: ground reaction through legs, trunk rotation, then the striking segment accelerates last and fastest. A jab (entry A07-02) and a front kick (entry A07-06) show the family's two shapes: straight-line, trunk-light strikes versus chambered, hip-driven strikes. Implement strikes (rackets, bats, clubs) add a lever: the implement extends the hand's radius of rotation, multiplying distal speed while demanding grip and timing precision.

**Throwing** is the classic kinetic chain. In an **overhead throw**, sequential ground reaction, hip and trunk rotation, then shoulder external rotation followed by rapid internal rotation, elbow extension, and wrist snap transfers momentum link by link, each proximal deceleration feeding the distal segment. The same chain, scaled differently, underlies dart, javelin, discus, and ball-throwing variants; catching reverses it into eccentric absorption.

**Athletics families** show the same principles at field scale. The **sprint start** from a crouch or block position pre-loads the hip and knee extensors and converts a four-point stance into horizontal triple extension. The **long jump** runs approach speed into a single-leg takeoff that converts horizontal velocity into a projectile arc, with flight styles (hitch-kick, hang) managing rotation before a two-foot landing (08 §5 mechanics). The **shot put** — glide or rotational styles at family level — is a put rather than a throw: the implement accelerates across the whole body path and releases forward-upward from a fully rotated, braced trunk.

## 6. Weightlifting and Resistance-Training Movement Families

Resistance movement forms a small orthogonal family. The **squat family** (bodyweight entry A05-04; back, front, and overhead variants) loads a descent-ascent pattern through hip-knee-ankle flexion-extension with the trunk pitched to keep the load over the feet. The **deadlift or hinge family** flexes primarily at the hips with a long, neutral spine, closing and opening the hip angle under load. The **press family** (overhead, bench) extends loads from the 06 §2 reach into loaded vertical or horizontal pressing.

The competitive weightlifting movements compress the whole chain into seconds. The **snatch** (entry A07-04) lifts the barbell from ground to overhead in one continuous motion, spending most of its bar speed on an explosive second pull from full triple extension, then catching the bar overhead in a deep squat before standing. The **clean and jerk** splits the task: a clean pulls the bar to the shoulders with the same explosive extension, and a jerk drives it overhead with a dip-and-drive leg push. Technique families (squat versus power catches, split versus squat jerks) vary legitimately; loads are technically regulated in competition, and this file records mechanics, not training prescriptions.

## 7. Swimming Strokes

Swimming replaces ground reaction with fluid reaction. Buoyancy offsets most of the body's weight, drag dominates the force budget, and propulsion comes from hands and forearms sweeping water backwards plus kick. The four regulated strokes are one family with four solutions. **Freestyle (front crawl)** alternates overarm cycles — entry, catch, pull with a curved underwater path, push, recovery over water — with a flutter kick and rolling body mechanics that link the arms; breathing rotates the head to the side within the roll (entry A07-03). **Backstroke** is the supine analogue with face-out breathing and a similar alternating cycle. **Breaststroke** is symmetric: simultaneous arm sweep and whip kick, with a glide phase in each cycle that makes it the slowest but most rhythm-dependent stroke. **Butterfly** undulates the whole body in a dolphin wave, drives simultaneous overarm recovery, and pairs both with a two-beat dolphin kick — mechanically the most demanding stroke, highest in peak power per cycle. Across strokes, the mechanical art is the same: shape the hand path to maximise effective propulsion while minimising form drag.

## 8. Martial Arts and Performance Acrobatics

**Martial arts movement families** reduce to striking, kicking, and grappling at mechanics level. Punching and kicking were covered in §5 (entries A07-02, A07-06); elbow and knee strikes shorten the striking radius and raise the mass behind contact. **Grappling** organises around off-balancing and leverage: a clinch or grip controls the partner's COM; a throw applies torque to move the partner's COM past their base of support rather than opposing their strength head-on; joint-lock techniques operate at form level here as controlled range-endpoint mechanics. Trained breakfalls — the receiving side of throws — are documented with fall mechanics in 08 §6.

**Performance acrobatics** inverts the usual relationship to gravity. A **cartwheel** (entry A07-05) is a lateral rotation through an inverted moment, each hand weight-bearing in turn. A **handstand** is static inverted balance: the COM must stack over a small base formed by the hands, with wrist, elbow, and shoulder making continuous corrections — balance control upside down, using the same sensory strategies of 08 §2. A **forward or backward roll** rounds the spine and spreads ground contact sequentially, converting a head-height descent into distributed, timed absorption — the same impulse-spreading logic as falling technique (08 §5-6) repurposed as a floor transition.

### A07-01 · Ballet plié

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Name | Ballet plié |
| Class | dance-technique |
| Definition | Controlled knee bend performed with turnout and a vertical, lifted trunk, heels grounded; the foundational bending element of ballet vocabulary. |
| Body segments | Both lower limbs, pelvis, trunk; arms in port de bras for balance. |
| Joint actions | Simultaneous hip external rotation and abduction (turnout within individual range), knee flexion tracking over the feet, ankle dorsiflexion with heel contact maintained (approximate, depth-dependent). |
| Phase structure | Descent (eccentric) → bottom of plié (brief stillness) → ascent (concentric) → vertical settle. |
| Temporal profile | ~2-4 s for a classroom grand plié cycle; petit plié faster (approximate). |
| Kinetic profile | Bodyweight loading through hip and knee extensors in eccentric-concentric alternation; depth multiplies knee and ankle demands; turnout torque resisted at the hips. |
| Context | Ballet class barre and centre; warm-up and vocabulary element across many dance forms. |
| Variation | Grand versus petit depth; achievable turnout varies with hip anatomy — range is individual, and the form trains within it rather than beyond it; individual proportions alter tracking. |
| Safety note | Descriptive mechanics only; dance training progressions belong to qualified instruction. |
| Sources | OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e* (chs. 9-11); NCBI Bookshelf (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books). |

### A07-02 · Boxing jab

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Name | Boxing jab |
| Class | sport-skill |
| Definition | Quick straight punch delivered with the lead hand from guard, extending along the line to the target and retracting immediately. |
| Body segments | Lead hand, forearm, elbow, shoulder; slight trunk and hip drive; footwork beneath. |
| Joint actions | Shoulder flexion and protraction, elbow extension to near-straight, wrist alignment to fist; small contralateral trunk rotation adds reach (approximate). |

### A07-03 · Swimming freestyle stroke cycle

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Name | Swimming freestyle stroke cycle (front crawl) |
| Class | sport-skill |
| Definition | Alternating overarm swimming stroke in which each arm sweeps a propulsive underwater path while the body rolls and a flutter kick stabilises progress, breathing rotated to the side. |
| Body segments | Both arms, shoulders, trunk with rolling mechanics, legs (flutter kick), head with rotational breathing. |
| Joint actions | Shoulder flexion into catch, elbow flexion in pull, extension in push, circumduction in recovery; ankle-pointed flutter kick from the hip; trunk roll couples the arms (approximate). |
| Phase structure | Per arm: hand entry → catch → propulsive pull (curved downsweep-insweep-upsweep path) → push → recovery over water; continuous flutter kick; breath rotation embedded per cycle or per two cycles. |

| Kinetic profile | Propulsion from hand and forearm path reacting against water; drag dominates the resistive budget; buoyancy carries most body weight; kick adds stabilisation and modest thrust. |
| Context | Pools and open water; sprint through distance events; recreational to elite. |
| Variation | Breath timing, kick cadence, catch depth, and roll amplitude vary by distance and swimmer; stroke rates differ widely within healthy performance. |
| Safety note | Descriptive mechanics only; water competence and supervision are real-world prerequisites outside this file's scope. |

### A07-04 · Snatch (weightlifting)

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Name | Snatch (weightlifting) |
| Class | sport-skill |
| Definition | Competitive weightlifting movement that raises a barbell from the ground to overhead in one continuous motion, catching it in a deep overhead squat before standing. |
| Body segments | Whole body; legs and hips generate the pull, trunk transmits, shoulders and arms fix the bar overhead. |
| Joint actions | First pull hip/knee extension; explosive second-pull triple extension (hip-knee-ankle); rapid shoulder flexion and elbow lock to receive overhead; deep squat with overhead shoulder flexion, then stand (approximate). |
| Phase structure | Setup → first pull (floor to knees) → transition → second pull (explosive extension) → turnover and catch → overhead squat → recovery to standing. |
| Temporal profile | Pull phase under ~1 s; full movement ~2 s (approximate). |

| Context | Competitive weightlifting and its training movements; technically regulated environments. |
| Variation | Squat versus power catch heights; grip width and pull geometry vary by lifter build; technique families differ legitimately within the rules. |
| Safety note | Descriptive mechanics only; maximal-load lifting is technically regulated and belongs to qualified coaching. |

### A07-05 · Cartwheel

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Name | Cartwheel |
| Class | acrobatic |
| Definition | Lateral rotation of the body through an inverted passage, weight-bearing travelling from one hand to the other while the legs swing overhead and land on the same side as they started. |
| Body segments | Both arms (successive weight-bearing), trunk, hips, both legs. |
| Joint actions | Shoulder flexion to overhead hand placement, elbow extension under brief single-arm support, hip flexion then extension as legs pass overhead, trunk lateral flexion through the passage (approximate). |
| Phase structure | Lunge or approach → first hand placement → second hand placement → inverted passage (hips over hands) → foot landing → rise to standing. |
| Temporal profile | ~1-1.5 s (approximate). |
| Kinetic profile | Momentum from the lunge carries the body through the inverted region; wrist and shoulder carry brief single-arm loads; landing reabsorbs a partial descent (08 §5 mechanics). |
| Context | Gymnastics, acrobatics, dance, informal play; mats or grass typical in training. |
| Variation | Speed, leg separation, arm straightness, and landing style vary; spotting and progression methods are standard in instruction and are not covered here. |
| Safety note | Descriptive mechanics only; inversion carries real practice risk and training belongs to qualified instruction. |
| Sources | OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e* (chs. 10-11); OpenStax *College Physics* (rotational motion). |

| Field | Value |

| Name | Karate front kick |

| Body segments | Support leg, kicking leg, hip, trunk. |
