# 04 · Human Anatomy and Physiology

Human anatomy and physiology supply the structural and functional substrate for every other human-domain file in this atlas: bones, joints, muscles, and nerves are the hardware on which locomotion (05), everyday actions and gestures (06), performance movement (07), and balance recovery (08) run. This file records that substrate at a descriptive, textbook level, organised from cells to systems, and states throughout how each structure relates to movement and function.

## 1. Scope, Method, and Safety Boundary

This file describes typical adult human structure and function as documented in open-licensed educational references, principally OpenStax *Anatomy and Physiology 2e* (CC BY 4.0), with anatomical concepts aligned to the Uberon multi-species anatomy ontology where stable term identifiers exist. Coverage is systematic: cells and tissues; the skeletal system and its bone groups; joints, ligaments, and anatomical planes; the muscular system; the nervous system; the circulatory, respiratory, digestive, endocrine, and metabolic systems; the immune, urinary, reproductive, integumentary, and sensory systems; and growth, development, ageing, and normal variation.

> **Safety note.** Everything here is descriptive structure-and-function knowledge. It is never diagnosis, treatment guidance, or clinical authority. Descriptions are population-level generalisations with explicitly marked normal variation; they do not characterise any individual. Questions about a real person's body or health belong with qualified health professionals, not with the atlas.

## 2. Cells and Tissues: The Body's Building Blocks

The body is composed of trillions of cells sharing a common plan: a phospholipid plasma membrane controlling what enters and leaves, cytoplasm holding the working machinery, and organelles with specialised roles (OpenStax A&P 2e, ch. 3). Mitochondria generate ATP by aerobic metabolism — the energy currency that every contraction, nerve impulse, and repair process spends. The nucleus stores genetic instructions; ribosomes and the endoplasmic reticulum build proteins, including the contractile and structural proteins that matter directly for movement.

Cells differentiate into four primary tissue classes (ch. 4). **Epithelial tissue** covers surfaces and lines cavities and glands, forming the skin's outer layer and the linings of airways and gut. **Connective tissue** is the movement-relevant class: it includes the dense regular connective tissue of tendons and ligaments, cartilage that cushions joint surfaces, bone, blood, and adipose tissue, all built from cells in a matrix whose properties (collagen for tensile strength, mineral for compression resistance) determine mechanical behaviour. **Muscle tissue** is contractile and occurs as skeletal (voluntary, attached to bone), cardiac (heart wall), and smooth (visceral walls) forms. **Nervous tissue** consists of neurons that conduct electrical signals and glial cells that support them. Sheets of connective tissue called fascia wrap muscles, organs, and compartments, transmitting force and allowing glide between structures.

## 3. The Skeletal System: Framework, Protection, and Leverage

The adult skeleton contains approximately 206 bones (the count varies with sesamoid and accessory bones) and serves five linked functions: structural support, protection of organs, leverage for muscle action, mineral storage (calcium and phosphate), and blood-cell production in red marrow (ch. 6). Bone is living, remodelling tissue: osteoblasts deposit matrix, osteocytes maintain it, osteoclasts resorb it, and bone architecture adapts over time to the loads habitually placed on it.

The skeleton divides into the **axial skeleton** (skull, vertebral column, thoracic cage) and the **appendicular skeleton** (limbs and girdles). The vertebral column (Uberon: UBERON_0001130) has 26 adult bones — seven cervical, twelve thoracic, five lumbar vertebrae, plus the fused sacrum and coccyx — balancing rigidity against a curve system that carries head and trunk loads down to the pelvis. The thoracic cage (12 rib pairs plus the sternum, UBERON_0000975) shields heart and lungs and contributes rib-motion capacity to breathing.

The appendicular skeleton is the movement apparatus proper. Each **pectoral girdle** pairs a clavicle (UBERON_0001105) with a scapula; the **upper limb** comprises the humerus (UBERON_0000976), the forearm's radius (UBERON_0001423) and ulna (UBERON_0001424), and the hand's 27 bones (8 carpals, 5 metacarpals, 14 phalanges) — the denser the distal skeleton, the finer the possible manipulation. The **pelvic girdle** (fused ilium, ischium, pubis) is the robust link between trunk and legs; the **lower limb** comprises the femur (UBERON_0000981) — the longest, strongest bone, engineered for weight-bearing — the patella, a sesamoid that improves quadriceps leverage, the tibia (UBERON_0000979) and fibula (UBERON_0000980), and the foot's 26 bones forming arches that store and return elastic energy in walking (05 §3). Bone classes track function: long bones act as levers, flat bones as armour and broad muscle sheets, short and irregular bones as load-transfer nodes.

## 4. Joints, Ligaments, Anatomical Planes, and Range of Motion

Joints are where movement is permitted, guided, or prevented (ch. 9). Fibrous joints (skull sutures, distal tibiofibular syndesmosis) permit little or no motion; cartilaginous joints (intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis) allow small cushioned movements; **synovial joints** are the mobile workhorses, each with articular cartilage on bone ends, a fibrous capsule, a synovial membrane secreting lubricating fluid, and reinforcing ligaments. Synovial subtypes map to movement demands: hinge joints (elbow, ankle) move in one plane; pivot joints (atlantoaxial) rotate; condyloid and saddle joints (finger knuckles, thumb base) move in two; ball-and-socket joints (shoulder, hip) move in all three. The mobility-stability trade is visible in the shallow, highly mobile shoulder versus the deep, restricted hip.

Ligaments connect bone to bone and provide passive restraint; they behave viscoelastically, resisting fast stretch more strongly than slow stretch. Range of motion at each joint reflects articulating-surface geometry, capsular and ligamentous slack, muscle bulk, and soft-tissue interference, and varies markedly between healthy individuals (see §9). Approximate healthy adult values illustrate the spread: elbow flexion roughly 135-150°, knee flexion roughly 130-150°, hip flexion roughly 110-125°, shoulder elevation approaching 180° only with scapular participation, ankle dorsiflexion roughly 10-20° and plantarflexion roughly 40-55°.

The atlas fixes a shared movement vocabulary from standard anatomical convention, and every action entry in files 05-08 uses it. The **anatomical position** (upright, palms forward) is the reference frame; the **sagittal, frontal (coronal), and transverse planes** with their axes partition motion. Movement terms: flexion/extension decrease or increase a joint angle in the sagittal plane; abduction/adduction move a segment toward or away from the midline in the frontal plane; medial/lateral rotation turn a segment about its long axis; circumduction combines these; special terms cover ankle and foot (dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, inversion/eversion), forearm (pronation/supination), and scapula (elevation/depression, protraction/retraction).

## 5. The Muscular System: Motors, Tendons, and Force Transmission

Skeletal muscle is the body's engine, with more than 600 named muscles converting chemical energy into tension (ch. 10; Uberon skeletal muscle tissue: UBERON_0001134). The contractile unit is the sarcomere: actin and myosin filaments slide past one another when calcium, released after a motor-nerve impulse, exposes binding sites — the sliding-filament mechanism. A **motor unit** (one motor neuron plus the fibres it innervates) is the functional address of control: small units in eye and finger muscles permit fine gradation, large units in postural leg muscles deliver force in coarse steps. Fibre types form a spectrum — slow oxidative (fatigue-resistant, endurance-oriented), fast oxidative-glycolytic, and fast glycolytic (high power, fatigue-prone) — and the mix differs between muscles and between individuals, a documented component of normal variation.

Muscle architecture trades excursion against force: parallel-fibred muscles (for example biceps brachii) shorten far; pennate muscles (for example the multipennate deltoid) pack more fibres into less excursion and produce more force. Within a movement, muscles take roles — agonist (prime mover), antagonist (opposing control), synergist, and stabiliser — so each joint action is simultaneously produced and braked. Eccentric (lengthening) contractions generate high tension at low metabolic cost and dominate controlled lowering, braking, and impact absorption (08 §5). Tendons, stiff collagen cables, transmit muscle force to bone and, at sites such as the Achilles tendon, store and return elastic energy during cyclic locomotion (05 §5). Major groups organise movement functionally: hip and knee extensors for support and propulsion, plantarflexors for push-off, trunk flexors/extensors/rotators for postural control, and the rotator cuff plus scapular muscles that steer the mobile shoulder.

## 6. The Nervous System: Command, Sensation, and Motor Control

The nervous system closes the loop between structure and movement (chs. 12-14). The **cerebral cortex** plans and issues voluntary movement: the frontal lobe carries premotor planning areas and the primary motor cortex with its somatotopic map; the parietal somatosensory cortex integrates touch and body position; occipital and temporal lobes deliver the vision and audition that guide reaching and balance. The **cerebellum** times and smooths movement, correcting error across repetitions — the substrate of motor learning; the **basal ganglia** participate in movement selection and initiation; the **brainstem** houses vital reflexes and cranial-nerve nuclei. The **spinal cord** (UBERON_0002240) conducts ascending and descending traffic and executes fast local circuits: the monosynaptic stretch reflex, operative within tens of milliseconds, stiffens a limb under unexpected load without waiting for cortical attention.

The **peripheral nervous system** carries mixed motor and sensory fibres — twelve cranial and thirty-one spinal nerve pairs — with conduction speeds scaling with fibre diameter and myelination. Specialised sensors report the body's state to itself: muscle spindles sense length and stretch velocity, Golgi tendon organs sense force, joint receptors signal extreme positions; this **proprioceptive stream** underlies position sense and balance (08 §2). Motor control blends feedforward programmes (anticipatory postural adjustments fire before a lifted load can disturb balance) with online feedback corrections, and improves with practice as movement becomes more consistent and more automatic.

## 7. Circulatory, Respiratory, and Metabolic Support of Movement

Movement is energetically expensive, and three systems supply it (chs. 18-20, 22-23). The **cardiovascular system** — heart (UBERON_0000948), vessels, and roughly five litres of blood — is a double circuit: the right heart drives blood through the lungs for oxygen loading; the left heart pushes it through the systemic circuit to working muscle. Resting cardiac output near five litres per minute can rise severalfold in sustained exertion. The **lymphatic system** returns escaped tissue fluid to the venous side and hosts immune surveillance. The **respiratory system** (UBERON_0002048; ch. 22) ventilates the alveoli: diaphragm contraction enlarges the thorax, alveolar pressure falls, air flows in; gas exchange follows partial-pressure gradients across the alveolar-capillary membrane, and haemoglobin shuttles oxygen to muscle.

Fuel arrives through the **digestive system** (ch. 23): carbohydrates, fats, and proteins are broken down and absorbed, with the liver buffering and distributing nutrients. Within muscle, three energy systems cover the demand spectrum — the phosphagen system for seconds of maximal effort, anaerobic glycolysis for tens of seconds to a few minutes, and aerobic oxidation for prolonged work — explaining why explosive actions (jumps, sprints, lifts) and endurance actions (distance running) differ in feel and in fuel. The **endocrine system** (ch. 17) regulates this economy: insulin and glucagon manage fuel availability, adrenaline mobilises acute readiness, cortisol mediates longer-term stress responses, and growth hormone supports tissue maintenance and repair.

## 8. Immune, Urinary, Integumentary, and Sensory Systems

The **immune system** defends through innate barriers and responses (skin barrier, inflammation, phagocytes) and adaptive learned responses (lymphocytes, antibodies); the atlas records only the structural baseline here. The **urinary system** — kidneys (UBERON_0002113), ureters, bladder — filters blood, conserving water and electrolytes while excreting wastes; fluid balance is directly relevant to sustained movement in heat. The **reproductive system** is documented at form level; its main movement relevance in this atlas is population-level sexual dimorphism (mean differences in stature and muscle-mass distribution, with wide overlap between groups — §9).

The **integument** (UBERON_0002097; ch. 5) is the body's largest organ: epidermis as barrier, dermis holding vessels, nerves, and glands, hypodermis as insulation and padding. Sweat glands make it the primary radiator for exertion thermoregulation, and dense mechanoreceptor populations in fingertips, palms, and soles feed grasp and foot-placement control.

The **sensory organs** construct perception (chs. 14, 16). The eye projects an inverted image onto the retina, where rods support dim-light vision and cones support colour and acuity; the visual system extracts motion, depth, and optical flow for guiding locomotion. The ear separates hearing (cochlea) from balance (vestibular apparatus): three semicircular canals sense angular head acceleration, and the otolith organs sense linear acceleration and head tilt relative to gravity — the body's fastest gravito-inertial reference, central to balance control (08 §2). Touch, proprioception, taste, and smell complete the multimodal stream that perception integrates into a single body-centred model of self and environment.

## 9. Growth, Development, Ageing, and Normal Variation

Structure is not static across life (ch. 28; OpenStax *Biology 2e* for developmental biology at form level). Long bones grow at epiphyseal plates until late adolescence; skeletal maturation and the pubertal growth spurt reshape leverage (limb-to-trunk ratios) and strength. Gross motor development follows a broadly consistent sequence — head control, sitting, crawling variants, assisted then independent walking — with wide, healthy variation in timing. Adulthood brings peak tissue performance followed by population-level ageing trends: gradual loss of muscle mass and strength (sarcopenia as a statistical trend), declining bone density, stiffer connective tissue, and slower speeded responses. These are averages with large spread; many older adults remain highly capable, and habitual activity shifts the curves.

Normal variation is the rule, not the exception, and the atlas marks it wherever numbers appear: stature and body proportions; joint laxity ranging from stiff to broadly hypermobile within the healthy spectrum; muscle fibre-type mix; foot arch height; sensory acuity; and trained versus untrained tissue adaptation. Every quantitative claim in this file and in files 05-08 must be read as a population-level approximation, never as a norm that individuals should meet.
