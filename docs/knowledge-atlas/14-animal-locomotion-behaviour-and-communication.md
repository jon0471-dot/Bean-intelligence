# 14. Animal Locomotion, Behaviour and Communication

## 1. Scope and the Clade-Level Pattern Rule

This file catalogues how animals move and communicate: terrestrial gaits, ricochetal and jumping locomotion, swimming, crawling and burrowing, gliding and powered flight, energy trade-offs, predator-prey pursuit, communication modalities, and the tracking-data source for movement evidence. The governing rule is anti-generalization: gait definitions and biomechanical details are stated at clade level, and one species' measured biomechanics is never projected onto all animals. Horse gaits are the classic, best-instrumented example and are marked species-specific; where other clades differ (pacing camels, non-galloping elephants), the difference is stated. Physics grounding comes from the physics guide; anatomical machinery from the anatomy and invertebrate guides.

## 2. Terrestrial Gaits: Walk, Trot, Canter, Gallop

Gaits are classified by footfall pattern and duty factor (fraction of stride a foot stays grounded). The walk is a four-beat lateral-sequence gait with duty factor above one half — at least two, usually three feet grounded. The trot moves diagonal limb pairs together as a two-beat gait near duty factor one half. The canter and gallop are asymmetric gaits: the canter has a three-beat pattern, the gallop a four-beat pattern with a suspension phase, occurring in transverse and rotary forms. Gait transitions are driven by speed and metabolic economy: in horses — the classic experimental system — animals change gaits near the speeds at which the next gait is energetically cheaper, an established result. Species-specific flags: footfall details and transition speeds here are established for horses and similar quadrupeds and must not be generalized; camels and some giraffes pace (lateral pairs move together); elephants maintain at least one foot on the ground even at their fastest speeds, with no aerial phase. These exceptions are established and block any single-species projection.

## 3. Bounding and Ricochetal Locomotion

Ricochetal (saltatorial) mammals such as kangaroos hop on enlarged hindlimbs with a stiffened tail for balance. The established mechanism is elastic storage: tendons of the hindlimbs stretch on landing and recoil at takeoff, returning energy that muscles would otherwise regenerate. Classic respirometry measurements showed that hopping oxygen consumption rises little across a range of speeds in large kangaroos — an established and distinctive result. Slow locomotion differs: kangaroos use pentapedal walking with the tail as a fifth support — established. Bounding (half-bounds and full bounds in small mammals) shares the elastic-recoil logic at smaller scale, with front-limb deceleration leading to hind-limb propulsion.

## 4. Jumping Specialists

Fleas jump via a cuticular catapult: resilin in the thoracic pleural arch stores muscle work slowly and releases it within milliseconds, producing accelerations far beyond direct muscle capability — established (mechanics detailed in the insect guide). Grasshoppers use the same logic with a cuticular catch and release in the hind femur. Frogs lengthen the takeoff with long hindlimbs; established studies of frog jumping show power outputs above direct muscle limits, implicating elastic recoil in addition to muscle pre-stretch. Kangaroo rats ricochet in desert scrub, combining saltation with predator evasion. The common physical principle — elastic energy storage — is grounded in the physics guide.

## 5. Swimming: Body and Caudal Fin Modes

Fish body-and-caudal-fin swimming is classified into established modes: anguilliform (whole-body undulation, as in eels), carangiform (undulation concentrated in the posterior half with a forked tail, as in jacks and trout), and thunniform (a stiff anterior body with oscillation localized to the caudal peduncle and a high-aspect-ratio lunate tail, as in tunas); median- and paired-fin paddling modes (including ostraciiform) cover slow, manoeuvrable swimmers — an established classification. Drag splits into friction and pressure components; established reductions include streamlined body profiles, mucus layers, and burst-and-glide strategies in some fish. Cetaceans converge on thunniform swimming from the mammalian side: horizontal flukes oscillated dorsoventrally generate lift-based thrust on both half-strokes — established. Penguins porpoise at speed: they leap in shallow arcs, briefly airborne, trading a little flight drag for breathing opportunities and reduced surfacing cost — an established behaviour.

## 6. Crawling, Slithering, Climbing and Burrowing

Snakes use four established locomotor modes, often switching by substrate: lateral undulation (pushing against environmental irregularities), sidewinding (on yielding sand; only two body regions contact the ground while intermediate segments are lifted), rectilinear locomotion (belly skin advanced in waves by costocutaneous muscles), and concertina movement (anchor-and-extend in narrow spaces). Geckos climb smooth surfaces with millions of setae bearing spatulate tips; adhesion is shear-dependent and dominated by intermolecular (van der Waals) forces — an established mechanism. Burrowing: mole forelimbs are broad outward-facing scoops; earthworms burrow by peristalsis against a hydrostatic skeleton (mechanism in the invertebrate guide); sandfish lizards "swim" through sand with an undulation measured in kinematic studies — established.

## 7. Gliding and Powered Flight

Gliders — sugar gliders and flying squirrels among them — stretch a patagium between fore- and hindlimbs; lift and drag balance weight along a shallow descent, and steering comes from limb and membrane tension adjustments plus tail control. Gliding is descriptive in this atlas: it costs less than flapping but loses altitude. Powered flight is not restated here: avian flight physics are treated in the dedicated flight guide, insect flight in the insect guide; bat flight — membrane wings spanning elongated fingers — is noted as the third independently evolved vertebrate flight system, established.

## 8. Energy Efficiency Trade-offs

Cost of transport — energy per unit body mass per unit distance — generally falls with body size across species, an established scaling pattern. Established economies include elastic storage (kangaroo tendons; locust-style insect catapults), buoyancy in water, and gait selection within a species' repertoire at speeds that minimize metabolic cost (horses as the classic evidence). Trade-offs run both ways: speed costs energy and endurance; armor, insulation and flight muscle mass all raise baseline costs. These are clade-level patterns; absolute values are species-specific and are not tabulated in this atlas.

## 9. Predator-Prey Pursuit Dynamics

Pursuit predators rely on sustained speed and manoeuvring (wolves, some raptors); ambush predators rely on concealment and short bursts (many cats). Prey responses include flight, crypsis, herding — where the confusion effect is a commonly invoked and partially supported mechanism — and stotting, the pronounced hopping of gazelles, for which signalling to predators is a supported hypothesis rather than a proven universal. Burst speeds are treated qualitatively throughout this atlas: measured top speeds are species-specific, context-dependent and instrument-sensitive, so no numeric league tables are recorded.

## 10. Communication Modalities

Five established modalities. Acoustic: oscine bird song is learned, with established dialects and cultural transmission; humpback whale songs show structured themes and population-level change — established. Visual: plumage and courtship displays, and the honeybee waggle dance (evidence status flagged in the insect guide). Chemical: pheromones, from long-distance moth sex pheromones to ant trail networks — established. Tactile: social grooming in primates builds bonds; elephant trunk contact coordinates groups — established behaviourally. Electric: weakly electric fish generate electric organ discharges for electrolocation and social signaling, and the jamming avoidance response shifts discharge timing to preserve sensing — an established neural mechanism. Where interpretation is debated (stotting function; dance-versus-odor weighting), the flag travels with the claim.

## 11. Tracking Data: Movebank

Movement evidence in this atlas (migration routes, home ranges, seasonal corridors) is anchored to Movebank, the established open platform for animal tracking data (GPS, Argos, accelerometer datasets). Movebank hosts study-level datasets published under per-dataset licenses; the atlas cites dataset licenses per the source register rather than assuming a single license. Cross-link: the physics of the tracked flight — winds, energy, navigation — is in the bird flight guide.
