# Languages, Communication and Social Behaviour

This file records the atlas vocabulary for human language — its structure, families, acquisition, writing systems and pragmatics — together with nonverbal communication, sign languages, and the fundamentals of social behaviour. It documents recognised patterns and definitions; it does not claim exhaustive coverage of the world's languages or of social-psychology research.

## 1. Scope and Method

Linguistics and the social sciences study phenomena that vary across individuals and cultures, so this file privileges definitions and mechanisms over enumerations. No counts of languages, speakers, or effect sizes are asserted where sources disagree; family names are given as recognised groupings, and specific empirical claims are stated with their evidence status. Communication research overlaps psychology and anthropology, and both lenses are used: the individual learner and the cultural community.

## 2. Language Structure

Linguists describe language on five connected levels:

- Phonology: the sound (or sign) inventory of a language and its patterning. Phonemes are contrastive units — /p/ and /b/ distinguish "pat" from "bat" — and languages differ in which contrasts they use, including stress and tone systems.
- Morphology: internal word structure. Morphemes are minimal meaning units, free ("book") or bound ("-s", "un-"). Inflection changes grammatical form ("walked"); derivation builds new words ("unbreakable" from "break").
- Syntax: the rules combining words into phrases and sentences. Structure is hierarchical, and word order varies across languages — verb-medial and verb-final orders are documented patterns, not deviations.
- Semantics: literal meaning — what expressions denote and how meanings compose. "The dog chased the cat" does not mean the same as its reversal, even with the same words.
- Pragmatics: meaning in context, including deixis ("here", "you"), implication, and speaker intent (Section 6).

The levels interact: a sentence is simultaneously a sound pattern, a morphological assembly, a syntactic tree, a semantic proposition, and a contextual act.

## 3. Language Families Overview

Languages related by descent from a common ancestor are grouped into families, established through systematic sound correspondences and shared innovations rather than surface resemblance. Widely recognised groupings include Indo-European, Sino-Tibetan, Afro-Asiatic, Niger-Congo, and Austronesian, with Dravidian, Turkic, Uralic, and others as further recognised families; some languages are isolates with no demonstrated relatives (Basque is a standard example), and some remain unclassified.

Three honesty rules apply: classification is evidence-based and revised as documentation improves; membership lists and internal trees differ between reference sources; and this atlas states no counts of languages or speakers, because such figures vary by source and definition. Family membership is a claim about historical descent, not about culture, race, or difficulty.

## 4. Language Acquisition

First-language acquisition follows broad, cross-linguistically documented stages: pre-linguistic cooing and babbling, single-word utterances, two-word "telegraphic" combinations, then rapid vocabulary growth with increasingly complex grammar. Comprehension typically runs ahead of production.

The critical-period hypothesis — that native-like mastery requires exposure early in life — is recorded with explicit nuance. Evidence from delayed first-language exposure and from second-language accent and grammar studies supports a sensitive period for some aspects of acquisition, particularly pronunciation and some grammar; the strong claim that everything must be learned within a fixed window is debated, the underlying mechanisms are not settled, and most evidence is observational rather than experimental. The atlas therefore states the hypothesis as "partially supported, mechanisms contested", never as a hard biological deadline.

Bilingualism is the global norm rather than the exception, and bilingual children reach language milestones in the same broad windows as monolingual children. Code-switching is a rule-governed practice, not a deficit.

## 5. Writing Systems

Writing systems are classified by what their basic symbols encode:

- Logographic: symbols encode words or morphemes; Chinese characters are the standard example.
- Syllabic: symbols encode syllables; Japanese kana is a standard example.
- Alphabetic: symbols encode individual consonant and vowel phonemes; the Latin and Cyrillic scripts are standard examples.
- Abugida: symbols encode consonants with vowels marked by diacritics or modification; Devanagari is a standard example.

Two cautions apply. First, writing is secondary to speech: many languages have no established writing tradition, and literacy is a learned technology, not a natural capacity. Second, real scripts mix principles — English orthography mixes alphabetic and morphophonemic conventions — so clean labels describe ideal types, and scripts are classified by their dominant principle.

## 6. Pragmatics and Speech Acts

Utterances do things as well as state things. Speech-act theory distinguishes the act of saying (locution), the act performed in saying (illocution — promising, requesting, warning), and the effect on the hearer (perlocution). Requests can be direct ("Close the door") or indirect ("It's cold in here"), and languages differ in how they conventionally soften or indirect requests.

Grice's cooperative principle is recorded conceptually: speakers are assumed to contribute appropriate quantity, quality, relation, and manner — the four maxims — and listeners derive implicature from apparent violations. "It's cold in here" seems to violate relation, so the listener infers a request. Politeness strategies and directness norms vary across cultures, so pragmatic interpretation requires cultural context, not only grammar — a point the atlas treats as central to any multilingual or cross-cultural system.

## 7. Nonverbal Communication

Face-to-face communication runs on channels beyond words:

- Kinesics: body movement, gesture, posture, and facial expression.
- Proxemics: use of interpersonal space; distance zones are conventional descriptions, and comfort distances vary by culture and relationship.
- Haptics: touch — greeting, affiliation, and status signalling, with strong cultural and contextual rules.
- Paralanguage: the vocal channel beyond words — pitch, rate, volume, and quality of voice.

Cross-cultural variation is the governing rule: the same gesture can carry opposite meanings in different regions, eye-contact norms invert between cultures, and display rules for emotion are learned. No nonverbal signal has a universal, context-free meaning, so the atlas records specific gesture meanings only with their cultural scope, and treats any "body language decoding" claim without scope as unsupported.

## 8. Sign Languages as Full Natural Languages

Sign languages used by deaf communities are full natural languages, a matter of established linguistics. They have phonology at the manual level (handshape, location, movement, orientation serving the role phonemes serve in speech), morphology and syntax of their own, regional and national varieties, and their own acquisition timelines: deaf children exposed to sign from birth acquire it on the same schedule as spoken language. Sign languages are not pantomime, are not universal — national sign languages differ and are often unrelated to the surrounding spoken language — and are not derived word-for-word from spoken languages. The atlas treats "sign language" as a class term only; claims about a specific sign language name its community, and invented universals about signing are recorded as false.

## 9. Social Behaviour Fundamentals

The social-psychology vocabulary the atlas uses covers: cooperation and its conditions (repeated interaction, reputation, common goals); reciprocity (direct, indirect, and generalised); norms, both descriptive (what people do) and injunctive (what people approve); personal and social identity, with in-group and out-group processes; and group dynamics such as conformity pressure and degraded decision-making in cohesive groups.

A standing caveat governs specific effect claims: the replication crisis in psychology — documented in the field itself — showed that many classic effects fail to replicate or appear much smaller than first reported. The atlas therefore does not state named single-study effects as facts. Any specific social-behaviour claim requires current evidence, reported effect sizes, and awareness of pre-registered replication results; where such evidence is absent, the claim is stated as a hypothesis or omitted. Norms and cooperation patterns with broad cross-cultural documentation are the safe core of this section.
