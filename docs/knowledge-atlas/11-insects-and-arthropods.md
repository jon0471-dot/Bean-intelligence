# 11. Insects and Arthropods

## 1. Scope

Arthropods ("jointed feet") are the largest animal phylum by described species. This file covers the shared arthropod body plan, insect-specific systems (head, thorax, wings), locomotion mechanics (walking, jumping, flight), colony behaviour and communication, metamorphosis, and the distinctions among insects, crustaceans, arachnids and myriapods. Species names are indexed against the GBIF backbone taxonomy with per-dataset licenses (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-NC); anatomical terms follow the Uberon multi-species anatomy ontology.

## 2. The Arthropod Body Plan: Segmentation, Cuticle, Ecdysis

Arthropod bodies are segmented, with segments grouped into tagmata: head, thorax and abdomen in insects; cephalothorax and abdomen in arachnids and in many decapod crustaceans. The exoskeleton is a layered cuticle: a thin epicuticle (wax- and cement-bearing, waterproofing) over a procuticle divided into hardened exocuticle and more flexible endocuticle. Chitin — a polymer of N-acetylglucosamine — forms fibers embedded in a protein matrix; sclerotization (quinone cross-linking, or "tanning") hardens the exocuticle, while unsclerotized cuticle at joints preserves articulation. Apodemes, internal cuticular ridges, serve as muscle attachment sites. Because a rigid cuticle cannot grow, arthropods molt: in ecdysis, apolysis separates the epidermis from the old cuticle, molting fluid digests and recycles the old endocuticle, a new cuticle is secreted beneath, and ecdysteroid hormones time the cycle. After the molt, the animal expands the new soft cuticle (by air intake and hemolymph pressure) before it hardens. The trade-off is explicit: armor and muscle leverage bought at the price of periodic vulnerability and desiccation risk.

## 3. Insect Body Segments; Compound Eyes and Antennae

The insect head carries feeding structures and the main sensory organs; the thorax (three segments) bears the legs and wings; the abdomen houses digestion, excretion and reproduction. Compound eyes are built of ommatidia, each a narrow optical channel with corneal lens, crystalline cone and a bundle of photoreceptor cells forming a rhabdom; the mosaic of ommatidial samples yields excellent motion detection. Apposition eyes (typical of diurnal insects) keep each ommatidium optically isolated; superposition eyes of many nocturnal insects pool light across ommatidia for sensitivity — an established distinction. Antennae are segmented and variably shaped (filiform, clubbed, pectinate) and carry sensilla for olfaction, mechanoreception, humidity and temperature; male moths detect female sex pheromones at extremely low concentrations with feathery antennae — an established sensitivity pattern.

## 4. Thorax, Legs and Leg Adaptations

The insect leg has the segment series coxa, trochanter, femur, tibia, tarsus (tarsomeres) and pretarsus with claws and adhesive pads. Functional adaptations are classic examples of form following use: jumping legs bear an enlarged femur housing the extensor mechanism (grasshoppers); raptorial forelegs of mantids have spined femora and tibiae that fold together like a jackknife; digging (fossorial) forelegs of mole crickets bear broad, toothed tibiae; swimming legs of diving beetles are flattened and fringed with hairs; worker bees carry a pollen basket (corbicula) on the hind tibia. These are clade- or species-specific modifications, not general arthropod features.

## 5. Wing Structures

Insect wings are double-layered epidermal evaginations stiffened by tubular veins that carry hemolymph and nerve supply; venation patterns are standard taxonomic characters. Structural types: membranous wings (flies, wasps — one or two pairs); elytra (beetle forewings hardened into protective covers for the folded hindwings); hemielytra (true bugs, forewings partly hardened); halteres (flies' reduced hindwings acting as gyroscopic sensors — campaniform sensilla in the haltere base detect Coriolis torques during body rotation, an established flight-stabilization mechanism). Wings fold fan-wise over the abdomen along flexion lines; in bees and wasps, rows of hooks (hamuli) couple forewing and hindwing into a single aerodynamic surface — established.

## 6. Insect Flight Mechanics

Two muscle systems power insect flight. Synchronous flight muscles contract once per nerve impulse per wingbeat (locusts and many larger insects), producing wingbeat frequencies in the tens of beats per second. Asynchronous ("fibrillar") flight muscles of flies, bees, beetles and true bugs are stretch-activated: sparse nerve impulses maintain the system, but the muscles oscillate at the mechanical resonance of the thorax-wing assembly, reaching hundreds of beats per second in small flies — an established distinction. Aerodynamically, the wings beat on a tilted stroke plane; both downstroke and upstroke contribute, with the angle of attack changing through the stroke. Unsteady mechanisms matter at insect scale: a leading-edge vortex augments lift, and in small insects the clap-and-fling at stroke reversal — wings touching and then peeling apart, drawing air in and enhancing circulation at the start of the downstroke — is an established lift mechanism. Force definitions (lift, drag, thrust) are grounded in the physics guide; the avian contrast is treated in the flight guide.

## 7. Walking: The Alternating Tripod Gait

Six legs allow the alternating tripod: front and rear legs of one side with the middle leg of the other form one triangle, the complementary three form the other, and the insect alternates between them. At low speed many insects use metachronal wave gaits; at higher speed the alternating tripod is the established standard fast gait, keeping at least three legs grounded for static stability while force measurements show dynamic components as speed rises.

## 8. Jumping: Elastic Catapults

Fleas store muscle work slowly in resilin — a rubber-like elastic protein — in the pleural arch of the thorax, then release it within milliseconds, achieving take-off accelerations far beyond direct muscle contraction. A long-standing debate over whether fleas push off with the trochanter or the tarsus was resolved by high-speed imaging showing tarsal ground contact — established. Grasshoppers pre-contract their hindleg extensors to store energy in the semilunar process of the femur and in apodeme cuticle, released through a catch mechanism — an established power-amplification design. Elastic-energy physics is grounded in the physics guide; vertebrate jumpers are compared in the locomotion guide.

## 9. Colony Behaviour and Communication

Eusocial colonies (ants, termites, honeybees and relatives) combine overlapping generations, cooperative brood care and reproductive division of labor. Ant trail pheromones recruit nestmates along concentration gradients, and termite cement pheromones coordinate construction — established chemical communication. The honeybee waggle dance communicates a food source: the waggle-run angle encodes direction relative to the sun compass and the waggle duration encodes distance — an established communicative function with decades of evidence. Evidence status: the informational content of the dance is established, while the relative weight of dance geometry versus odor cues in recruitment remains a subject of published research discussion and is stated here as an ongoing refinement rather than a settled ratio. Alarm, aggregation and sex pheromones operate across insects as an established modality.

## 10. Metamorphosis and Life Stages

Three patterns: ametabolous development (primitively wingless insects; juveniles resemble adults), hemimetabolous (egg, nymph, adult; wings grow as external pads — "incomplete" metamorphosis), and holometabolous (egg, larva, pupa, adult; internal wing primordia and imaginal discs proliferate during pupation, and larval tissues are reorganized — "complete" metamorphosis). Endocrine control is established: ecdysteroids initiate each molt, while juvenile hormone maintains larval identity; a falling juvenile hormone titer permits the metamorphic molt. A widely supported adaptive advantage of holometabolous development is that larvae and adults exploit different resources, reducing competition within a species.

## 11. Non-Insect Arthropods: Crustaceans, Arachnids, Myriapods

Crustacea (crabs, shrimp, copepods, isopods): two pairs of antennae, typically biramous appendages, many with planktonic nauplius larvae; decapod cuticle is mineralized with calcium carbonate. Arachnida (spiders, scorpions, mites, ticks): no antennae; cephalothorax and abdomen; four pairs of walking legs; chelicerae and pedipalps; respiration by book lungs and/or tracheae; Malpighian tubules for excretion, shared with insects. Myriapoda: millipedes have two leg pairs per diplosegment and are detritivores with defensive secretions; centipedes have one leg pair per segment, are predators, and inject venom through forcipules (modified first legs) — an established distinction between the two groups.
