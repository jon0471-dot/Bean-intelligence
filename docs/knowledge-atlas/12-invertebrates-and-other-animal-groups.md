# 12. Invertebrates and Other Animal Groups

## 1. Scope

This file covers the major non-arthropod, non-vertebrate animal groups: molluscs, annelids, cnidarians, echinoderms, sponges, flatworms and roundworms, with a dedicated section on locomotion mechanics across these groups. Species names are indexed against the GBIF backbone taxonomy (per-dataset licenses CC0, CC BY, CC BY-NC); anatomical terms follow the Uberon multi-species anatomy ontology. Statements are clade-level patterns with species-specific exceptions flagged.

## 2. Molluscs: Gastropods, Bivalves, Cephalopods

The molluscan body plan combines a muscular foot, a visceral mass and a mantle that often secretes a shell; most molluscs have a radula (a rasping feeding organ) and an open circulatory system, with cephalopods the established exception. Gastropods (snails, slugs) undergo torsion: during larval development the visceral mass rotates roughly 180 degrees relative to the head-foot — an established developmental process whose selective advantage is still discussed in the literature. Bivalves (clams, mussels, scallops) are laterally compressed, with two valves hinged by a ligament and closed by adductor muscles; they lack a radula and filter plankton with ciliated gills, drawing water in and out through siphons. Cephalopods (squids, octopuses, cuttlefish, nautilus) modify the foot into arms and a funnel (siphon), run a closed circulatory system with accessory branchial hearts, and possess camera-type image-forming eyes that are convergent with vertebrate eyes, not homologous.

## 3. Cephalopod Nervous Systems and Chromatophores

Cephalopods have the largest and most centralized nervous systems among invertebrates: a lobed brain encircling the esophagus, large optic lobes, and arm ganglia that support substantial local control in octopus arms. Squid giant axons — unusually thick, fast-conducting nerve fibers — became classic material for experimental neurophysiology; their existence and use are established. Skin colour is produced by chromatophores: organs containing a pigment sac that is rapidly expanded or contracted by radial muscle fibers under direct neural control, giving millisecond-scale colour and pattern change; iridophores and leucophores add reflective and white components. Camouflage matching of substrate patterns, deimatic displays and signaling are established uses. The mechanisms above are established; higher-level claims about cephalopod cognition are treated as an active research area in this atlas.

## 4. Annelids: Segmentation and the Hydrostatic Skeleton

Annelids (polychaetes, earthworms, leeches) are metameric: the body repeats coelomic compartments separated by septa, with segmental nerves, nephridia and muscles. The hydrostatic skeleton works because coelomic fluid is incompressible: circular muscle contraction narrows a segment and drives elongation at constant volume; longitudinal muscle contraction shortens and widens it; the two layers act antagonistically. Septal compartmentalization lets the worm localize shape changes, producing the peristaltic waves used in burrowing and crawling — an established mechanical account. Setae (chaetae) anchor segments against the substrate; polychaetes add parapodia with gills or paddle-like swimming surfaces. Circulation is typically closed, with dorsal and ventral vessels; respiration is cutaneous in moist earthworms and branchial or parapodial in many polychaetes.

## 5. Cnidarians: Cnidocytes, Polyps, Medusae, Corals

Cnidarians are diploblastic sac-shaped animals with a nerve net and no centralized brain in most forms. Their defining feature is the cnidocyte, a stinging cell containing a capsule (nematocyst) with a coiled tubule; discharge is triggered mechanically or chemically and is driven by high osmotic pressure inside the capsule, making it one of the fastest movements known in cells — established. The phylum's two body forms alternate across the life cycle in many species: the sessile polyp and the motile medusa; anthozoans (anemones, corals) lack the medusa stage — established. Corals: scleractinian (stony) polyps secrete calcium carbonate skeletons; reef-building depends on endosymbiotic dinoflagellates (zooxanthellae) that supply photosynthate to the host, enabling high productivity in nutrient-poor water. Coral bleaching — expulsion of symbionts under thermal stress, starving the coral — is an established stress response.

## 6. Echinoderms: The Water Vascular System

Echinoderms (sea stars, sea urchins, sea cucumbers, crinoids) are deuterostomes with a hydraulic locomotor system. The water vascular system runs from the madreporite (a sieved intake opening) through the stone canal to the ring canal, then radial canals, lateral canals and ampullae, ending in tube feet. Contraction of an ampulla raises hydraulic pressure and extends the tube foot; longitudinal foot muscles retract and steer it; adhesion comes from foot-tip glands and suction — an established mechanical description. Adult pentaradial symmetry is secondary: larvae are bilateral, so the five-fold body plan evolved within a bilateral deuterostome lineage. The endoskeleton consists of calcareous ossicles, and mutable collagenous tissue allows echinoderms to stiffen or soften connective tissue with little muscular energy — an established, distinctive property.

## 7. Sponges: Filter Feeding Without True Tissues

Poriferans lack true tissues, organs, nerves and muscles; their bodies are organized at the cell level. Choanocytes — collar cells with beating flagella — line internal chambers and pump water through incurrent pores (ostia), canal systems and the excurrent osculum, filtering bacteria and particles. The skeleton combines spicules (calcium carbonate or silica, taxonomically diagnostic) and spongin fibers. Archaeocytes are totipotent cells; dissociated sponge cells can reassemble into a functioning sponge — an established demonstration of cellular-level organization. Water flow rates are generated entirely by choanocyte flagella, making sponges the atlas's baseline example of filter feeding without organs.

## 8. Flatworms and Roundworms: Platyhelminthes and Nematoda

Platyhelminthes are acoelomate, dorsoventrally flattened worms; without circulatory or respiratory organs, they rely on diffusion, which the flat shape makes viable. Protonephridia with flame cells handle osmoregulation; digestion runs through a branched gastrovascular cavity. Free-living planarians show chemosensory head lobes and prominent regeneration; parasitic flukes and tapeworms carry established adaptations including a scolex with suckers or hooks, proglottids for egg production, and a tegument resistant to host digestion. Nematodes are pseudocoelomate roundworms with a collagenous cuticle that is shed by ecdysis (placing them in Ecdysozoa), only longitudinal muscles (hence sinusoidal undulation rather than peristalsis), and extremely high abundance in soils, sediments and detrital food webs; established representatives include root-knot plant parasites and animal parasites such as Ascaris and hookworms.

## 9. Locomotion Across Groups

Jet propulsion: cephalopods refill the mantle cavity when relaxed, then contract the muscular mantle to expel water through the funnel; funnel orientation steers the jet. Scallops swim in short bursts by rapidly clapping their valves, expelling water at the hinge margins — established mechanics in both cases. Peristalsis: earthworms propagate alternating circular- and longitudinal-muscle contractions along the body against a hydrostatic skeleton, with setae providing anchorage — see the annelid section above for the mechanism. Tube-foot stepping: sea stars right themselves and crawl by coordinating hundreds of tube feet whose extension is hydraulic and whose retraction is muscular. Undulation: nematodes swim and crawl with sinusoidal waves produced by longitudinal muscles against a pressurized, stiff cuticle. Ciliary gliding: small flatworms move over surfaces on ciliary beating with mucus lubrication. Each mode trades speed for economy differently; quantitative comparisons belong to the physics and locomotion guides.
