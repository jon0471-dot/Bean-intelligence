# 09. Animal Kingdom: Taxonomy and Evolution

## 1. Scope and Working Method

This file is the atlas entry point for metazoan (animal) diversity: the major phyla, the body-plan features used to compare them, the current understanding of their phylogenetic relationships, the evolutionary principles that generate diversity, and the taxonomy practice used to name and index species. Statements are written at clade level: a pattern attributed to a phylum or class holds for the group as a whole, and species-level exceptions are flagged where they change the mechanism. Species names in this atlas are indexed against the GBIF backbone taxonomy, which aggregates species-occurrence records contributed by data publishers under per-dataset licenses (CC0, CC BY, or CC BY-NC); GBIF is a record backbone and name index, not a nomenclatural authority. Anatomical terminology follows the Uberon multi-species anatomy ontology, and textbook-level grounding is OpenStax Biology 2e, with NCBI resources used for taxonomic and bibliographic verification.

## 2. Major Metazoan Phyla

Animals are multicellular, ingestive heterotrophs without cell walls, typically with collagen-supported extracellular matrices and developmental patterning genes shared across the kingdom. Nine phyla carry most of the atlas's animal content.

Porifera (sponges): sessile filter feeders with no true tissues or organs; water is moved through choanocyte-lined canals; support comes from spicules or spongin. Cnidaria (jellyfish, anemones, corals): diploblastic, radially symmetric animals characterized by stinging cnidocytes and life cycles built around polyp and medusa forms. Platyhelminthes (flatworms): bilateral, dorsoventrally flattened, acoelomate worms without circulatory or respiratory organs; many are parasites. Nematoda (roundworms): pseudocoelomate worms with a sheddable cuticle; among the most abundant animals in soils and sediments, including major parasites of plants and animals. Annelida (segmented worms): true coelomic metameres, circular and longitudinal muscle layers, and typically a closed circulatory system; includes polychaetes and clitellates (earthworms, leeches). Mollusca (snails, clams, squids): muscular foot, visceral mass and mantle; most have shells and an open circulatory system, with cephalopods the striking exception. Arthropoda (insects, crustaceans, arachnids, myriapods): jointed appendages, segmented bodies organized into tagmata, and a chitinous exoskeleton shed by ecdysis; the largest phylum by described species. Echinodermata (sea stars, sea urchins): deuterostomes with a water vascular system driving tube feet and pentaradial adult symmetry. Chordata: defined by a notochord, dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits and a post-anal tail at some stage of life; contains the vertebrates.

## 3. Body Plans: Symmetry, Germ Layers, Coelom, Segmentation

Comparative body plans rest on four axes. Symmetry: sponges are largely asymmetric; cnidarians and adult echinoderms are radially symmetric (in echinoderms this is secondary, since their larvae are bilateral); the remaining phyla are bilaterally symmetric, a precondition for directed movement and cephalization. Germ layers: diploblastic animals (cnidarians) build bodies from ectoderm and endoderm; triploblastic animals add mesoderm, enabling muscle, circulatory tissue and complex organs. Coelom: triploblastic bodies range from acoelomate (platyhelminthes, with mesoderm filling the space between gut and body wall), through pseudocoelomate (nematodes, a cavity not fully lined by mesoderm), to true coelomate (annelids, molluscs, echinoderms, chordates); arthropods retain a reduced coelomic contribution and an open hemocoel. Segmentation: repetition of body units (metamerism in annelids, repeating segments with tagmatization in arthropods, vertebral repetition in chordates) allows regional specialization on a repeatable construction kit; it is a recurring evolutionary solution, not by itself evidence of close kinship.

## 4. Protostome and Deuterostome Development

Bilateral animals divide by early developmental logic. In protostomes the blastopore, the first opening of the gastrula, becomes the mouth; cleavage is typically spiral and determinate. In deuterostomes the blastopore becomes the anus, cleavage is radial and indeterminate, and the coelom forms by outpocketing of the archenteron. Protostomes subdivide into Ecdysozoa (animals that molt a cuticle: arthropods, nematodes) and Lophotrochozoa (molluscs, annelids, flatworms and allies). Deuterostomes comprise echinoderms, hemichordates and chordates. These categories are developmental tendencies rather than guarantees: cleavage patterns vary among lineages, so modern classification leans on molecular phylogenies rather than on any single embryological trait.

## 5. Phylogenetic Relationships: Modern Understanding

Molecular phylogenies combined with morphology give a broadly stable picture: sponges branch near the base of Metazoa; cnidarians are sister to the Bilateria; bilaterians split into deuterostome and protostome branches; and within protostomes the Ecdysozoa–Lophotrochozoa split is well supported. Chordates sit with echinoderms and hemichordates inside Deuterostomia, which is why sea stars and humans share deuterostome development. The fossil record brackets this diversification: Ediacaran-aged assemblages preserve early large-bodied animal forms, and the Cambrian explosion documents the rapid appearance of most extant phylum-level body plans. Flagged as active research: whether sponges or comb jellies (Ctenophora) are sister to all other animals remains contested, with published support on both sides; the placement of several deep bilaterian lineages (for example Xenacoelomorpha) still shifts as data accumulate. Atlas rule: deep-node position statements are provisional and are updated rather than asserted as settled.

## 6. Evolutionary Principles and Speciation

The core is descent with modification: populations change because heritable variation is generated (mutation, recombination) and filtered. The principal mechanisms are natural selection (non-random differential reproductive success of heritable variants, producing adaptation), genetic drift (random change in allele frequencies, strongest in small populations), gene flow (movement of alleles between populations) and non-random mating. Selection is described as directional, stabilizing or disruptive depending on how fitness varies across a trait. Speciation occurs when populations acquire reproductive isolation: prezygotic barriers (temporal, behavioral, mechanical, gametic) prevent mating or fertilization, while postzygotic barriers (hybrid inviability or sterility) reduce hybrid fitness. Geographic context distinguishes allopatric speciation (barrier-separated populations) from sympatric speciation (divergence within a shared range, for example through host shifts or, in plants, polyploidy). Evidence classes include the stratified fossil record, homologous structures, biogeographic distributions, and molecular sequence phylogenies that independently recapitulate morphology-based trees in most well-sampled groups.

## 7. Taxonomy Practice: Ranks, Binomials, Cladistics, and the GBIF Backbone

The Linnaean hierarchy arranges taxa as domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species, with registered intermediate ranks where needed. Binomial nomenclature names each species by genus plus species epithet in italics, governed for animals by the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature. Modern classification is cladistic: named groups should be monophyletic clades diagnosed by shared derived characters (synapomorphies), inferred by parsimony, maximum-likelihood or Bayesian analysis of morphological and molecular character matrices; molecular data dominate modern animal phylogenetics. Paraphyletic labels such as "invertebrates" persist as convenience terms, not as clades. Practically, this atlas treats GBIF as the working species index: occurrence records and the reconciled backbone resolve which names are in current use and where taxa are recorded, while nomenclatural authority remains with the code and the taxonomic communities; occurrence data carry per-dataset licenses (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-NC) that must be respected when records are reused. NCBI Taxonomy provides the sequence-linked complementary classification.

## 8. Evidence Status and Confidence

High confidence: monophyly of the major phyla; the Ecdysozoa–Lophotrochozoa split; deuterostome composition; the Cambrian timing of most body plans. Contested (active research): the deepest split in Metazoa (sponge-first versus ctenophore-first hypotheses); exact branching order among several deep lineages; molecular-versus-morphological conflicts in a few placements. Interpretive cautions: a "body plan" is an organizational summary, not a rigid constraint; homology must be distinguished from analogy before any evolutionary claim; and taxonomy changes over time, so atlas statements cite the backbone index rather than freezing a name list.
