# 15. Plants, Fungi, Microbes and Ecosystems

## 1. Scope

This file covers the non-animal biological domains the atlas needs for grounded generation: plant structure and transport, photosynthesis, plant movements, fungi, microbes, and the ecosystem-level concepts (energy flow, nutrient cycles, succession, keystone species, biodiversity-stability). It contrasts with the animal guides: plants are sessile autotrophs, fungi are absorptive osmotrophs, microbes span prokaryotic and eukaryotic life. Textbook grounding is OpenStax Biology 2e; NCBI resources verify taxonomy and bibliography.

## 2. Plant Structure: Roots, Stems, Leaves

Vascular plants organize into three tissue systems — dermal (outer protective epidermis), ground (cortex, pith, mesophyll), and vascular — repeated across organs. Roots anchor, store, and absorb; the root tip carries an apical meristem and a protective root cap, behind which zones of elongation and maturation differentiate epidermis (with root hairs), cortex, and vascular cylinder. Stems support and connect, carrying vascular bundles that in dicots arrange as a ring (enabling secondary growth via cambium) and in monocots as scattered bundles. Leaves are the primary photosynthetic organs: a blade with stomata (adjustable pores) on the epidermis, palisade and spongy mesophyll for light capture and gas exchange, and veins linking the leaf to the whole-plant transport network.

## 3. Vascular Transport: Xylem, Phloem and the Cohesion-Tension Mechanism

Xylem conducts water and minerals from roots to leaves through dead-at-maturity, lignified cells — tracheids and, in angiosperms, vessel elements. The accepted transport model is the cohesion-tension mechanism: evaporation in mesophyll air spaces lowers water potential, tension pulls the water column, and hydrogen-bond cohesion plus adhesion to cell walls sustains it; stomatal guard cells regulate the flux by adjusting turgor through ion uptake. Cavitation (air embolism breaking the water column) is an established risk the system works around; fine-scale details of embolism repair remain researched, so the model is stated as the accepted textbook mechanism rather than a closed question. Phloem transports sugars through living sieve-tube elements (each serviced by companion cells); transport follows the established pressure-flow model — osmotic loading at sources builds turgor that pushes sap toward sinks.

## 4. Photosynthesis: Light Reactions and Carbon Fixation

The light reactions run in thylakoid membranes: photosystem II splits water (releasing oxygen), electrons travel through plastoquinone, the cytochrome complex and plastocyanin to photosystem I and onward to reduce NADP+ to NADPH; electron transport pumps protons into the thylakoid lumen, and ATP synthase converts the resulting gradient into ATP (chemiosmosis) — established. The Calvin cycle fixes carbon in the stroma: RuBisCO attaches CO2 to ribulose bisphosphate, the products are reduced using the light-reaction ATP and NADPH into triose phosphates, and the cycle regenerates its acceptor — established. Two established adaptations reduce photorespiration and water loss: C4 plants fix CO2 temporarily into four-carbon acids in mesophyll cells and release it inside bundle-sheath cells (maize, sugarcane), and CAM plants fix CO2 at night, storing acids until daytime (cacti, pineapple).

## 5. Plant Movements: Tropisms, Nasties and Carnivorous Traps

Tropisms are directional growth responses: phototropism (established auxin redistribution producing differential elongation), gravitropism (established statolith sedimentation in specialized cells as the sensing step), thigmotropism (contact-guided coiling of tendrils). Nastic movements are directional-independent: the sensitive plant Mimosa pudica folds leaflets by thigmonasty — motor cells in the pulvinus rapidly lose turgor after ion efflux, and the signal propagates electrically and chemically through the leaf — an established mechanism. Carnivorous plants combine movement with nutrition: the Venus flytrap snaps shut when its trigger hairs are stimulated twice within a short window, with the trap closing through a rapid geometry change of the curved lobes — an established mechanism; sundews ensnare with sticky tentacles; bladderworts suck prey in with a suction trap that is among the fastest movements in the plant kingdom — established; pitchers are passive pitfall traps with digestive fluid.

## 6. Fungi: Hyphae, Spores and Mycorrhizal Symbiosis

Fungi are osmotrophs: hyphae — tubular filaments with chitin-rich cell walls — grow by tip extension and form mycelial networks that secrete extracellular enzymes and absorb the digested products; white-rot fungi's ability to break down lignin is established and ecologically central. Spore dispersal uses established mechanics: ballistospores jump by the surface-tension coalescence of a condensation droplet (the established Buller's-drop mechanism); ascus cells squirt spores by pressure release; puffballs release clouds when compressed by rain or animals. Mycorrhizal symbiosis couples fungi to plants: in arbuscular and ectomycorrhizal associations, the fungus receives plant photosynthate and supplies the plant with mineral nutrients (especially phosphorus and nitrogen) — an established mutualism that involves the majority of land plant species and anchors soil nutrient economies.

## 7. Microbes: Bacteria, Archaea, Protozoa and Biofilms

Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes — no membrane-bound nucleus or organelles — and the two domains differ in established ways, including archaeal ether-linked, branched membrane lipids and many archaeal extremophile lifestyles. Protozoa are eukaryotic heterotrophs moving with cilia, flagella or pseudopodia. The bacterial flagellum is a rotary motor: a stator assembly anchored in the cell envelope drives the rotor (basal-body rings) using ion gradients (protons or sodium ions), and switching of rotation direction mediates the run-and-tumble swimming of established chemotaxis behaviour — the motor mechanism is established. Biofilms: surface-attached microbial communities embedded in an extracellular matrix, with established quorum sensing — population-density signaling via secreted autoinducers — coordinating gene expression, and established antibiotic tolerance differences from planktonic cells.

## 8. Ecosystem Energy Flow and Trophic Levels

Ecosystems organize energy flow through trophic levels: producers (plants, algae, some microbes) capture solar energy; primary, secondary and higher consumers feed on them; decomposers recycle the remainder. Energy flow is unidirectional, with a large fraction lost as heat at each transfer; the classic "ten percent" rule is recorded here as a rough pedagogical heuristic for average ecological efficiency, not a law — real transfer efficiencies vary widely with taxa and environment. The distinction between energy (flows once, exits as heat) and matter (cycles) organizes the next section.

## 9. Nutrient Cycles: Carbon, Nitrogen, Water

The carbon cycle links photosynthetic fixation, respiration, ocean uptake and organic-matter burial; fossil-carbon combustion adds an anthropogenic flux — established. The nitrogen cycle depends on microbial transformations: nitrogen fixation by diazotrophs converts N2 to ammonia using the oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase enzyme (established, hence the specialized protective niches of fixers), followed by nitrification, assimilation, ammonification and denitrification back to N2; industrial fixation adds a major anthropogenic flux — established. The water cycle couples evaporation, transpiration, precipitation, runoff and groundwater storage; plant transpiration links the water and carbon cycles through stomatal behaviour described above.

## 10. Succession, Keystone Species and Biodiversity-Stability

Succession describes community change after disturbance: primary succession on bare substrate begins with pioneers (lichens, hardy plants) building soil; secondary succession follows disturbance where soil persists; facilitation, inhibition and tolerance are the established conceptual models, and the modern view treats "climax" as a dynamic equilibrium rather than a fixed endpoint. Keystone species exert effects disproportionate to their abundance: sea otter predation on sea urchins protects kelp forests and starfish predation maintains intertidal diversity are the classic, established examples. Biodiversity-stability relationships carry an evidence nuance: many experiments support positive diversity-productivity and diversity-stability correlations, but the underlying mechanisms (portfolio effects, insurance hypotheses) are context-dependent — effect size and direction vary with scale, taxa and environment, and the atlas states them with that qualification rather than as absolutes.
