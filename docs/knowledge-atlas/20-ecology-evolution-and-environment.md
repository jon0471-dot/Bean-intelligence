# 20. Ecology, Evolution and Environment

Ecology explains how living things interact with each other and with their physical surroundings; evolution explains how the diversity of life arose and keeps changing. This guide records the established mechanisms of both, plus the environmental interactions between human activity and ecosystems, marking heuristics and active research explicitly.

## 1. Scope and role in the atlas

The guide covers ecological organisation levels, population dynamics, community ecology, energy flow and biogeochemical cycles, evolution and its evidence, biodiversity and its threats, and human-environment interactions. It grounds itself in the physical domains: climate and terrain from earth science (19), thermodynamic limits from physics (16), nutrient chemistry from chemistry (17).

## 2. Ecological levels of organisation

Ecology is studied at nested levels. The organism is a single living individual. A population is a group of one species living and interbreeding in an area. A community is all populations of different species interacting in that area. An ecosystem adds the physical environment and its flows of energy and matter through the community. A biome is a broad regional system — tropical forest, grassland, desert, tundra — defined by characteristic climate and vegetation, which is why biome maps largely mirror climate maps from the earth guide (19). Each level has its own characteristic questions: individuals cope with conditions, populations grow or shrink, communities shift in composition, ecosystems process energy and nutrients, biomes respond to climate.

## 3. Population dynamics

Population growth is described by two idealised models. Exponential growth: dN/dt = r·N, where N is population size and r the per-capita growth rate — unlimited growth that accelerates without bound. Logistic growth adds a ceiling: dN/dt = r·N·(K − N)/K, where K is the carrying capacity — the population the environment can sustain; growth slows as N approaches K because resources, space or other limits bite harder at higher density. Real populations overshoot, crash and fluctuate around K rather than glide onto it.

The r/K selection idea, used conceptually, sorts life histories along a spectrum: r-selected species reproduce fast, mature early and invest little per offspring (many weeds and insects), while K-selected species invest heavily in fewer offspring and dominate where competition near carrying capacity matters (large mammals). It is a heuristic contrast, not a rigid classification.

Predator-prey interaction is captured, conceptually, by the Lotka-Volterra model: predators increase when prey is abundant and prey declines when predators are numerous, producing coupled oscillations — abundant prey, then rising predators, then prey crash, then predator crash, then prey recovery. The textbook model is an idealisation: real cycles depend on habitat refuges, alternative foods, time lags and environmental randomness, so field data often show damped or irregular cycles. The mechanism (interaction-driven oscillation) is established; exact cycle prediction is not.

## 4. Community ecology: interactions and niches

Species interactions are classified by effect. Competition (both harmed) occurs over shared limited resources; competing species may exclude one another or diverge in resource use. Predation and herbivory (one benefits, one harmed) shape populations and drive defensive adaptations. Symbiosis covers close associations: mutualism benefits both (pollinators and flowers), commensalism benefits one without affecting the other, parasitism benefits one at the other's expense.

The niche is a species' functional role and resource profile: the fundamental niche is where it could persist, the realised niche is where it actually persists given competitors and predators. Keystone species are those whose effect on the community is far larger than their abundance — their removal restructures the whole system; foundation species create habitat that many others depend on, as corals and trees do. Succession describes directional community change after disturbance: primary succession colonises bare substrate (new lava or rock), secondary succession recovers disturbed but soil-bearing land, typically passing from fast colonisers toward slower, competitive, longer-lived species — though disturbance can reset the path at any time.

## 5. Energy flow and matter cycling

Energy enters ecosystems almost entirely as sunlight fixed by photosynthesis, then flows upward through trophic levels: producers, then consumers, then predators, with decomposers recycling dead material at every level. Because respiration, movement and heat loss consume most of the energy taken in, only a fraction passes to the next level — commonly summarised by the 10 percent rule. This rule is a heuristic, not a law: transfer efficiency varies widely, but the consequence is robust, so trophic pyramids are narrow at the top and top predators are rare. The ultimate reason is thermodynamic (16): energy degrades to heat as it passes through organisms, so energy flows one way, while matter cycles.

Matter does cycle. The carbon cycle moves carbon among atmosphere (mostly carbon dioxide), living biomass, soils, fossil reservoirs and the ocean, which exchanges carbon directly with the air; photosynthesis and respiration are the fast loop and geological storage the slow loop. The nitrogen cycle requires nitrogen-fixing organisms to convert atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms, which then move through food webs and back to the air via denitrification. The water and phosphorus cycles complete the set; their physical side — rainfall, runoff, weathering — is documented in the earth guide (19), and the biological machinery of fixation, respiration and photosynthesis belongs to the biology domain guide (file 15). Ecology composes these pieces into ecosystem budgets.

## 6. Evolution: mechanism and evidence

Evolution is change in the genetic composition of populations over generations; the mechanism is natural selection, stated as a logical chain: individuals vary; much variation is heritable; resources are limited, so survival and reproduction are not random; individuals whose inherited traits fit the conditions leave more offspring; hence favourable traits become more common in the next generation. Selection acts on individuals, but populations evolve — a standard misconception is otherwise.

Genetic variation arises from mutation (changes in DNA sequence) and recombination (shuffling in sexual reproduction). Beyond selection, evolution is shaped by genetic drift — random fluctuation of gene frequencies, strongest in small populations, which can fix or lose variants without any fitness advantage — and by gene flow, the movement of genes between populations through migration. Speciation occurs when populations stop exchanging genes: allopatric speciation follows geographic separation; sympatric speciation occurs within a shared range, for instance through habitat or behavioural differentiation.

The evidence is convergent across independent lines: the fossil record documents past life and transitions; comparative anatomy shows homologous structures shared by descent; molecular biology shows universal genetic code and gene-sequence relationships matching anatomical history; and evolution has been observed directly in laboratory and field populations, including antibiotic resistance. Common misconceptions to avoid: evolution is not goal-directed; "more evolved" is meaningless; individuals do not evolve; and "theory" in science means a well-supported explanatory framework, not a guess.

## 7. Biodiversity: measurement, threats, conservation

Biodiversity has three levels: species diversity (richness — how many species — combined with evenness of their abundances), genetic diversity within species (the raw material of adaptation), and ecosystem diversity. It is measured by sampling: counts of species and their relative frequencies in standardised plots or surveys.

The principal threats interact. Habitat loss and degradation — conversion of land to agriculture, settlement and infrastructure — is the largest. Invasive species establish outside their native ranges and outcompete or prey on natives. Overharvesting depletes populations faster than they can recover. Pollution degrades air, water and soil. Climate change shifts temperature and rainfall zones faster than many species can move or adapt. Each threat acts on the population mechanisms of section 3: lowered birth rates, raised death rates, shrinking carrying capacities.

Conservation approaches work at the same levels: protected areas and habitat corridors preserve populations and permit movement; restoration ecology repairs degraded systems; captive breeding and seed banks conserve genetic resources ex situ; sustainable-use management aligns harvest with regrowth; and legal instruments restrict trade and pollution. Conservation effectiveness depends on the community and ecosystem concepts of sections 4 and 5 — protecting a keystone or foundation species often preserves far more than its own numbers.

## 8. Human-environment interactions

Resource use divides into renewable flows that regenerate on human timescales (forests, fisheries, soil fertility if managed) and non-renewable stocks that do not (fossil fuels, mined minerals); sustainability means harvesting renewable flows no faster than regrowth and substituting for depleted stocks. Pollution classes follow the medium and mechanism: air pollution (particulates and reactive gases), water pollution (nutrient runoff driving eutrophication — algal blooms and oxygen depletion — plus contaminants), soil degradation, and persistent materials such as plastics.

Ecosystem services classify what nature provides: provisioning services (food, water, timber, fibre), regulating services (climate regulation, flood control, water purification, pollination), cultural services (recreation, aesthetic and spiritual value) and supporting services (soil formation, nutrient cycling). The classification exists because unpriced services are lost when decisions count only marketed outputs. The planetary mechanism behind the largest current pressure — greenhouse-driven climate change — is recorded in the earth guide (19); its ecological consequences express themselves through the population and community mechanisms of this guide.
