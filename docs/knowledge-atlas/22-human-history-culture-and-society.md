# Human History, Culture and Society

This file records the shared vocabulary and recurring patterns the atlas uses for human history, culture, social structure, economics, political organisation, religion, and the history of science. It documents broad patterns and scholarly conventions, not a chronicle of events, and does not claim exhaustive coverage of any civilisation, region, or period.

## 1. Scope and Method

History is reconstructed from evidence. Historians distinguish primary sources — documents, inscriptions, artefacts, and records produced in or near the period under study — from secondary sources, which interpret primary evidence. Every category in this file (period names, social typologies, economic categories) is a modern scholarly convention used for comparison and communication; it is not a claim that people in the past organised their world in these terms. Dates given here are broad conventions with deliberately fuzzy boundaries, and boundary dates vary by region and by author. Where a claim is contested among historians, this atlas records it as contested rather than asserting one side.

## 2. Historical Periodization Overview

A widely used — and explicitly conventional — periodisation divides human history into prehistory (before surviving written records), ancient history (the first several millennia of writing), the classical period of particular regions, the medieval period, the early-modern period, and the modern period from roughly industrialisation onward. Three honesty rules apply whenever these labels are used:

- Period boundaries are conventions, not events. Transitions between periods were gradual and regionally uneven.
- The labels originate in the study of particular regions (for example, "medieval" was coined for European history) and transfer imperfectly to other regions, which carry their own periodisations.
- Period labels describe chronology, not worth. "Ancient" and "medieval" carry no judgement of sophistication or quality.

Prehistory is studied through archaeology rather than texts, which changes what can be claimed: material remains support statements about technology, diet, and settlement far more reliably than statements about beliefs, names, or motives.

## 3. Culture: Material and Symbolic

Anthropologists commonly split culture into material culture — tools, buildings, clothing, and other physical products — and symbolic culture — language, values, norms, beliefs, and practices. Culture is transmitted through learning rather than genes: vertically from parents, horizontally among peers, and obliquely through teachers and institutions. Transmission explains both continuity across generations and change across decades.

A long-standing debate concerns whether major innovations spread mainly by diffusion (borrowing between societies) or by independent invention. The evidence supports both mechanisms: agriculture emerged independently in several regions, while many technologies demonstrably travelled along trade routes. For any specific claim of borrowing or independence, the atlas requires regional evidence rather than assumption; neither mechanism is a default explanation.

## 4. Social Structures

Human societies organise themselves through three overlapping structures:

- Kinship: descent rules, marriage systems, and household forms determine inheritance, alliance, and obligation in most historical societies. Kinship terminology is a core comparative tool in anthropology.
- Class and stratification: societies differ in how much status is ascribed (assigned at birth) versus achieved (earned), and in how rigid the boundaries between strata are.
- Institutions: durable rule systems — law, education, religion, markets, government — coordinate behaviour beyond face-to-face groups and outlive their founders.

These structures interact. Institutions often encode kinship or class advantages, and changes in one structure (for example, industrial wage labour) reshape the others, which is why social change is studied as a system rather than a single variable.

## 5. Economics Overview

Economics begins from scarcity: unlimited wants meet limited resources, forcing choices whose trade-offs are the discipline's subject matter. Across history several recurring institutions appear in different forms:

- Trade and exchange: reciprocity, redistribution, and market exchange are the classic modes of moving goods between people.
- Money: a medium of exchange, store of value, and unit of account. Money made multi-party exchange, taxation, and long-distance trade scalable.
- Markets: price-coordinated exchange. For most of history markets coexisted with households, guilds, and states rather than dominating production.

Industrialisation — a conventionally dated transformation beginning in the late eighteenth century in parts of Europe — reorganised production around inanimate energy, machinery, and factories. Its recorded consequences, including urbanisation, wage labour, mass consumer goods, and sustained economic growth, transformed every social structure listed above. This is why industrialisation is treated as a dividing line in periodisation and why its spread is described region by region rather than as a single date.

## 6. Political Organization

Anthropology classifies political forms by scale and complexity: bands (small, kin-based, relatively egalitarian forager groups), chiefdoms (hereditary leadership over multiple communities with redistribution), and states (centralised authority with law, bureaucracy, and a monopoly on legitimate force), with tribe-like forms between bands and chiefdoms. This typology is a comparative convention, not a ladder of progress: societies are not "stages", many do not fit neatly into one category, and the typology has been criticised and refined since its introduction. It is used here only as a vocabulary for scale, not as a ranking.

For modern systems, democracy and authoritarianism are treated as ideal types — simplified poles describing where decision authority and accountability lie — rather than binary classifications. Real governments mix features: elections with unequal influence, monarchies with democratic institutions, republics with unelected power centres. Any claim about a specific country's classification requires describing its actual institutions, not applying a label.

## 7. Religion and Belief Systems

This atlas treats religion functionally and neutrally: belief systems are recorded as social facts that address meaning, morality, community identity, ritual life, and explanations of the world. It distinguishes organised religion (institutions with doctrine and clergy), local and folk practice, and secular worldviews, and it makes no claims about the truth of any belief. Respect rules follow from this framing: traditions are described in their own terms where sources allow, categories from one tradition are not imposed on another, and internal diversity within traditions is acknowledged rather than flattened. The same evidencing rules apply as elsewhere in the atlas: describe what is documented, attribute contested claims as positions, and avoid invented specifics about any tradition's practice, texts, or numbers.

## 8. Science History Arc

The atlas summarises the history of science as a three-phase arc, using conventional labels:

- Natural philosophy and craft knowledge: observation, practical technique, and mathematics existed for millennia, but knowledge claims were rarely tested against nature by a shared, self-correcting method.
- The scientific revolution (sixteenth to seventeenth centuries, by convention): observation, mathematical description, and experiment were combined into a social practice — instruments, journals, replication, and organised dissent — producing the modern institution of science.
- Industrial and digital eras: science became coupled to engineering and industry, and later to computation and large-scale data, accelerating both measurement and modelling.

The mechanism worth retaining is institutional, not heroic: science works through methods, publication, replication, and criticism, which is what makes self-correction possible. Technology and engineering inherit from this arc; their modern practice is detailed in [24-technology-engineering-and-computing.md](24-technology-engineering-and-computing.md).

## 9. Historiography Honesty

Because history is evidence-based reconstruction, its claims carry the limits of their evidence, and the atlas states those limits:

- Survival bias: written sources overrepresent elites, cities, and literate contexts; archaeology partially corrects this, but only for what material remains can show.
- Perspective and bias: every source has an author with interests. Corroboration across independent sources strengthens a claim; single-source claims are marked as such.
- Revisionism is normal: re-examining evidence with new methods or questions is how historical knowledge improves. It differs from denialism, which rejects evidence in favour of a preferred conclusion.
- No invented detail: names, dates, motives, and quotations are recorded only when evidenced. Uncertainty is stated explicitly ("conventionally dated", "disputed") rather than hidden.

These rules make historical content auditable: any claim can be traced to a source type, and contested claims are attributed as positions rather than asserted as facts.
