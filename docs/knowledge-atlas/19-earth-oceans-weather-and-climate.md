# 19. Earth, Oceans, Weather and Climate

Earth is the physical environment every living system in this atlas inhabits. This guide records the geology, oceanography, meteorology and climate science needed to ground ecosystems, human movement in terrain and planetary comparisons, keeping established mechanism separate from active research and from policy debate.

## 1. Scope and role in the atlas

The guide covers the rock cycle and minerals, plate tectonics, geologic time, ocean structure and circulation, the atmosphere and the greenhouse mechanism, weather systems, the climate system including the scientific position on climate change, and the water cycle. It grounds ecology (20), consumes physics (16) and chemistry (17), and supplies the planetary reference point for astronomy (21).

## 2. Geology: the rock cycle and minerals

Minerals are naturally occurring crystalline solids of definite chemical composition — quartz, feldspar, calcite — and rocks are aggregates of them. The rock cycle transforms rock through three families. Igneous rock forms when magma or lava cools and solidifies: fast cooling gives fine-grained basalt, slow cooling gives coarse-grained granite. Sedimentary rock forms when weathered debris is transported, deposited in layers, then compacted and cemented — sandstone, shale, limestone (often from shells and other biological material). Metamorphic rock forms when existing rock is altered by heat and pressure without melting — limestone to marble, shale to slate. The cycle runs because plate motion, uplift and erosion constantly move material between surface and interior environments; weathering itself is chemistry (17) applied to mineral surfaces.

## 3. Plate tectonics: boundaries, earthquakes, volcanism

Earth's rigid outer shell is broken into plates riding on the hotter, slowly flowing mantle beneath. At divergent boundaries plates separate and new crust forms along mid-ocean ridges. At convergent boundaries plates collide: oceanic crust subducts beneath other crust, feeding volcanoes and building mountain belts as crust thickens and folds. At transform boundaries plates grind past each other, as along the San Andreas fault system.

Earthquakes occur when rock that has been slowly strained by plate motion suddenly slips along a fault — the elastic-rebound mechanism: elastic energy stored over decades releases in seconds as seismic waves. Volcanism concentrates at subduction zones and spreading ridges, and also at mantle hotspots far from plate edges, such as the chain of Hawaiian volcanoes. Mountain building results from collision-driven thickening and folding. Plate tectonics is the unifying theory of geology and is established science.

## 4. Geologic time

Earth is about 4.5 billion years old. The geologic time scale divides deep time into eons, eras and periods defined by the fossil record and major events, from the Precambrian (most of Earth's history, dominated by microbes) through the Paleozoic, Mesozoic and Cenozoic eras. Absolute ages come from radiometric dating: radioactive isotopes decay at fixed, element-characteristic rates, so parent-to-daughter ratios in minerals measure time — the chemistry of section 2 of the chemistry guide (17) applied as a clock. Deep time matters conceptually: it is the frame in which plate motion, mountain building and evolution (20) operate.

## 5. Oceans: structure, currents, tides, chemistry

The ocean is one connected, stratified body of water covering about 71 percent of Earth's surface, organised by depth zones from sunlit surface waters to dark abyssal depths. Surface currents are wind-driven: prevailing winds push the upper ocean, and Earth's rotation deflects that motion (the Coriolis effect) into large gyres circulating around the ocean basins. Deep circulation is thermohaline, driven by density: cold, salty water forms at high latitudes, sinks, and spreads through the deep ocean in a global conveyor that slowly redistributes heat and dissolved gases.

Tides are generated by gravity. The Moon's pull raises two bulges on Earth — one toward the Moon, one on the opposite side caused by the difference in pull across the planet — and Earth's rotation carries coastlines through the bulges, typically producing two high and two low tides per day. The Sun's tide-generating force is roughly 46 percent of the Moon's; when Sun and Moon align (new and full moon) the tides reinforce as spring tides, and when they are at right angles the tides diminish as neap tides.

Ocean water averages about 3.5 percent dissolved salts, buffered near pH 8 by the carbonate system (17). The El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is the dominant year-to-year climate variation: weakened Pacific trade winds let warm surface water spread eastward (El Niño), shifting rainfall and temperature patterns worldwide, with the cold-water counterpart called La Niña.

## 6. Atmosphere: layers and the greenhouse mechanism

The atmosphere is stratified by temperature. In the troposphere (surface to roughly 8–15 km) temperature falls with height at an average environmental lapse rate of about 6.5 °C per kilometre, and nearly all weather occurs. In the stratosphere temperature rises with height because ozone absorbs ultraviolet sunlight; the mesosphere cools again; the thermosphere warms dramatically as sparse molecules absorb extreme solar radiation.

The greenhouse effect is established physics and is distinct from debates about climate policy. Sunlight is mostly shortwave radiation, which passes through the atmosphere and warms the surface. The warm surface re-emits that energy as longwave infrared radiation. Water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and other greenhouse gases absorb infrared and re-emit it in all directions, including back downward, so the surface and lower atmosphere stay warmer — about 33 °C warmer globally — than an airless Earth would be. Without this mechanism the surface would be largely frozen; with too much trapping, as on Venus (21), it is hostile. The mechanism is not controversial; the open scientific questions concern magnitude and pace under rising greenhouse-gas concentrations, discussed in section 8.

## 7. Weather: air masses, fronts, storms

Weather is short-term atmospheric state. Air masses — vast volumes with uniform temperature and humidity — form over source regions and carry those properties with them. Where air masses meet, fronts form: cold fronts drive under warm air, forcing rapid uplift and often brief intense storms; warm fronts ride over cooler air, producing longer, steadier precipitation.

Precipitation requires rising air. Convection — surface heating making buoyant air rise — cools the air to its dew point, water vapour condenses on tiny particles (condensation nuclei) into cloud droplets, and droplets grow until they fall. Latent heat released by condensation (17, section 7) is the energy engine of strong convection. Cyclones are low-pressure systems with counterclockwise circulation in the Northern Hemisphere (clockwise in the Southern), where converging, rising air sustains cloud and rain; anticyclones are high-pressure systems with sinking air and settled weather. Thunderstorms are convective cells powered by moist unstable air and latent heat, producing lightning and sometimes hail. Tornadoes develop from severe thunderstorms where wind shear organises a rotating column of air. Hurricanes (tropical cyclones) form over warm ocean water — roughly 26 °C or warmer — extracting energy from evaporation and latent-heat release as they rotate around a calm eye; they weaken over land or cold water where the energy supply is cut.

## 8. Climate system and the position on climate change

Climate is the long-term statistical behaviour of weather. Its first-order pattern is set by latitude: sunlight strikes the tropics nearly overhead and the poles at grazing angles, so a surplus of energy near the equator and deficit at the poles drives the atmospheric and oceanic circulation that transports heat poleward (16, 19). Altitude matters through the lapse rate: high ground is colder. The ocean couples to climate through its enormous thermal inertia — it damps and delays change — and through modes like ENSO. Feedbacks amplify or damp change: the ice-albedo feedback works because bright ice reflects sunlight while dark open water absorbs it, so melting begets more melting; water-vapour feedback strengthens warming because a warmer atmosphere holds more of the strongest greenhouse gas. Over longer spans, Milankovitch cycles — slow changes in orbital eccentricity, axial tilt and precession with periods on the order of tens to hundreds of thousands of years — pace the ice ages.

On anthropogenic climate change, the scientific position can be stated at textbook level: greenhouse-gas concentrations have risen well above pre-industrial levels from burning fossil fuels and changing land use, the resulting energy imbalance warms the planet, and the world's major science academies concur that observed warming is substantially human-caused. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) is the international body that periodically assesses and summarises this evidence; this atlas cites that role generically rather than quoting specific assessment figures. Actively researched areas, flagged as such: narrowing the equilibrium climate-sensitivity band (how much warming follows a given forcing), cloud feedbacks, ice-sheet dynamics and regional projections — each an assessed range rather than a settled single number.

## 9. The water cycle

Solar energy drives a continuous loop: evaporation from ocean and land lifts water as vapour; transpiration releases it from plants; condensation forms clouds; precipitation returns it as rain, snow, sleet or hail; on the ground it runs off toward streams and rivers or infiltrates soil and rock to become groundwater flowing slowly to wells, springs and streams. The cycle stores water in reservoirs — ocean, ice, groundwater, surface water, atmosphere and living things — and moves energy with the water: latent heat released and absorbed at each phase change is a major carrier of the atmosphere's energy budget, linking this section back to storms (section 7) and to thermodynamics (16).
