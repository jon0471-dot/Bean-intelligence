# 21. Astronomy and Space

Astronomy extends the physics of guide 16 to the largest scales: planets, stars, galaxies and the universe as a whole. This guide records the observational basics, solar-system structure, stellar evolution, galactic and cosmological context, planetary science and the physics of spaceflight — separating established results from ongoing research wherever the boundary matters.

## 1. Scope and role in the atlas

The guide covers observational basics, the solar system, stellar evolution, galaxies, cosmology, planetary science and space-exploration physics. It grounds itself in classical mechanics, waves and gravitation from physics (16), contrasts planetary environments with Earth (19), and applies the engineering constraints of materials and spacecraft systems (18).

## 2. Observational basics

Position on the sky is described on the celestial sphere, an imagined globe centred on the observer: declination measures angular position north or south of the celestial equator, right ascension measures angular position around it — a coordinate system fixed to Earth's rotation, so stars appear to wheel nightly while staying in fixed relative patterns. Brightness is measured on the magnitude scale, inherited from antiquity and now defined logarithmically: a difference of five magnitudes equals a brightness factor of 100, so m2 − m1 = −2.5·log10(F2/F1); smaller numbers mean brighter objects, and the scale extends to negative values for very bright bodies and to large values for faint telescopic objects.

Telescopes do two things: gather more light than the eye (light-collecting power grows with aperture area) and resolve finer detail (resolution improves with aperture and worsens with wavelength). Refractors focus with lenses; reflectors with mirrors, which dominate modern large telescopes because mirrors can be supported from behind and segmented. Earth's atmosphere distorts and absorbs much of the spectrum, motivating mountaintop sites, adaptive optics and space telescopes.

## 3. The solar system

The solar system formed, by the nebular hypothesis, from a collapsing cloud of gas and dust: conservation of angular momentum (16, section 6) flattened the infalling material into a spinning disk, the centre collapsed into the Sun, and planets accreted from disk material — explaining why the planets orbit in nearly the same plane and direction. Interior to the frost line, rocky and metallic material condensed into the terrestrial planets (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars); beyond it, ices and gas built the giant planets (Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune). Leftover debris populates the asteroid belt (rocky fragments between Mars and Jupiter), the Kuiper belt (icy bodies beyond Neptune) and the Oort cloud inferred far beyond; comets are icy planetesimals that grow tails of sublimated gas and dust when solar heating acts near the Sun. Moons orbit planets — hundreds are known — ranging from captured asteroids to geologically active worlds.

Orbital mechanics follows Kepler's three laws: (1) planets move on ellipses with the Sun at one focus; (2) a line from Sun to planet sweeps equal areas in equal times, so planets move faster when closer; (3) the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, T² = k·a³ — in convenient units, P² = a³ with P in years and a in astronomical units for bodies orbiting the Sun. Newton's gravitation (16, section 7) explains all three laws, and the same physics produces the tides that Moon and Sun raise on Earth's oceans (19, section 5).

## 4. Stellar evolution

Stars form where clouds of gas and dust collapse under gravity into dense, warming cores; when the core temperature suffices, hydrogen fusion ignites and the contraction stops. A star then spends most of its life on the main sequence, fusing hydrogen into helium in its core while gravity is balanced by gas pressure — hydrostatic equilibrium. Fusion releases energy because fused products have slightly less mass than the reactants; the mass difference appears as energy according to Einstein's mass-energy relation, the physics underlying stellar luminosity. The Sun, a middle-aged main-sequence star, is roughly halfway through a total main-sequence life of about ten billion years.

Fate is set by mass. Low- and medium-mass stars exhaust core hydrogen, swell into red giants, eventually shed their outer layers, and leave behind a white dwarf — an Earth-sized ember supported against gravity by electron degeneracy pressure, slowly cooling. High-mass stars (above roughly eight solar masses, an approximate boundary) fuse heavier elements at ever-faster rates, build an iron core that cannot release fusion energy, and collapse; the rebound powers a supernova that disperses synthesized elements — the source of much of the material in planets and people. The collapsed remnant becomes a neutron star (supported by neutron degeneracy, an object of extreme density) or, if the core exceeds a critical mass (about two to three solar masses, approximate), a black hole whose gravity prevents light from escaping its event horizon. Element synthesis in stars and supernovae is established; details of late-stage processes remain active research.

## 5. Galaxies

Galaxies are gravitationally bound systems of hundreds of billions of stars, gas, dust and dark matter. Morphologically they divide into spirals (flattened disks with arms and active star formation), ellipticals (smooth spheroids of older stars) and irregulars (shapeless, often disturbed systems). The Milky Way is a barred spiral: a disk of spiral arms where star formation continues, a central bulge surrounding a supermassive black hole, and a sparse halo of old stars and globular clusters; the Sun sits in a disk arm roughly halfway out, orbiting the galactic centre over hundreds of millions of years.

Galaxy rotation is the classic evidence for dark matter: stars and gas orbit too fast for the visible matter's gravity, and gravitational lensing and cluster dynamics agree, so the working conclusion is that most matter is dark — interacting gravitationally but not electromagnetically. Dark matter is inferred, not directly detected; its particle nature is an active research frontier, and this guide marks it as such rather than as settled fact.

## 6. Cosmology

The universe is expanding. Hubble's law states that a galaxy's recession speed is proportional to its distance, v = H0·d, measured through the redshift of galaxy spectra — the Doppler (16, section 12) and space-stretching effect that shifts light to longer wavelengths; the measured proportionality H0 is near 70 km/s per megaparsec, a value still being refined, with the refinement itself an active measurement programme. The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is radiation released when the early universe cooled enough for neutral atoms to form and light to travel freely; observed today as a nearly uniform microwave glow with tiny fluctuations, it is the strongest single piece of evidence for the hot big-bang model.

The big-bang timeline, in overview: from extreme early conditions the universe expanded and cooled; a hypothesised early inflationary phase smooths and flattens it (inflation is well-supported conceptually but its mechanism remains debated); light nuclei formed in the first minutes (nucleosynthesis, which matches observed light-element abundances); atoms formed hundreds of thousands of years later, freeing the CMB; galaxies and stars condensed over following hundreds of millions of years. Observations of distant supernovae show the expansion is accelerating; the standard explanation is dark energy — a label for whatever drives that acceleration. Dark energy is a mix of hypothesis and current consensus: the acceleration is observationally established, while the underlying physics is unknown and actively researched, as is the dark-matter question of section 5.

## 7. Planetary science

Planetary atmospheres compare instructively. Venus, with massive carbon-dioxide atmosphere and thick clouds, exhibits a runaway greenhouse far hotter than its distance from the Sun would otherwise allow; Mars retains only a thin carbon-dioxide atmosphere and cold surface conditions; Titan, Saturn's large moon, holds a dense nitrogen-methane atmosphere with surface liquid hydrocarbons; Earth alone maintains liquid-water oceans, oxygen-rich air and a temperature moderated by a modest greenhouse (19, section 6). The differences trace atmosphere mass, composition and planetary history rather than sunlight alone.

Habitability factors are the conditions for life as known: liquid water within a temperature range set by distance from the star and greenhouse balance; an energy source; essential elements; and, for long-term stability, sufficient atmosphere and protection from radiation. The habitable-zone concept marks the orbital band where surface liquid water is possible.

Exoplanets are detected mainly by three established methods. The transit method detects the periodic dip in a star's brightness when a planet crosses its disk; it yields planet radius and orbit size, and enables atmosphere studies through starlight filtering through the planet's atmosphere. The radial-velocity method detects the Doppler shift of the star's spectrum as the planet's gravity makes it wobble; it yields mass estimates. The astrometric method measures the same wobble as tiny shifts of the star's position on the sky. Together these methods have established that planets are common, with many systems unlike our own — an observational result of the last decades, with characterization of small rocky planets still advancing.

## 8. Space exploration physics

Rocket flight obeys the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, stated conceptually: the achievable velocity change is Δv = ve·ln(m0/mf), where ve is exhaust speed, m0 initial total mass and mf final mass after propellant is spent. Because the gain grows with the logarithm of the mass ratio, most of a launch vehicle's starting mass must be propellant, and staging — discarding empty tanks — keeps the ratio favourable. Rockets move by expelling mass backward: Newton's third law (16, section 3), not any push against air.

Orbits are free-fall trajectories at speed: low Earth orbit (LEO) sits a few hundred to about two thousand kilometres up, circling in roughly ninety minutes and used for imaging and many satellites; geostationary orbit (GEO) sits about 35,800 km above the equator, where the orbital period matches Earth's rotation so a satellite hovers over one longitude, used for communications and weather imaging. Between them lie transfer orbits — paths such as the Hohmann transfer, an elliptical orbit tangent to two circular ones that carries a spacecraft from one to the other with two engine burns, the fuel-efficient standard for raising orbits and reaching other planets.

A gravity assist uses a moving planet to change a spacecraft's speed and direction: in the planet's frame the flyby is symmetric, but in the Sun's frame the spacecraft gains or loses the planet's own orbital momentum, letting missions reach the outer planets with far less propellant — the technique that enabled the grand-tour trajectories of the outer solar system. Spacecraft engineering then applies the materials, thermal-control and electronics constraints of guide 18.
